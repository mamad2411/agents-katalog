# Crystalize

## Purpose

Crystalize is an artistic reconstruction workflow, not a crystal/glass filter
or a crystal-object generator.

**Source photograph → visual analysis → extraction → simplification → recomposition → selective crystallization → art direction**

The source photograph is visual raw material, not a composition template. Preserve its visual identity and story, not its complete photographic composition.

> **Hard failure:** If the result is a crystal sculpture, floating island,
> mineral specimen, 3D diorama, or “the original photograph made of crystal,”
> the transformation has failed.

> **Hard rule:** Do not perform one-to-one scene translation.

> **Hard rule:** Preserve the source orientation by default. Portrait sources
> produce portrait artwork; landscape sources produce landscape artwork. Change
> orientation only when the user explicitly asks.

## Workflow

### 1. Observe

Ask: if this photograph became a minimalist artwork, what must remain
for the scene to still be recognizable?

### 2. Extract

Identify approximately **2–5 visual anchors**: a primary subject, only the secondary forms that support it, and any atmospheric cue needed for recognition or story. Aggressively remove incidental photographic information.

Use `references/composition.md` for the extraction and layout decisions.

### 3. Simplify

Important elements retain recognizable silhouettes, proportions, colors,
and defining details. Everything else may become a broad shape, atmospheric mark, matte field, or negative space.

### 4. Recompose as an Illustration

Default to a **single partial editorial vignette** on warm ivory or cream paper,
using the source's portrait or landscape orientation. Keep only enough spatial
story for recognition, then invent a new arrangement through scale shifts,
cropping, spacing, overlap, shape reduction, and negative space. Aim for an art
magazine, travel journal, or sketchbook plate—not a formal advertising poster.
The result must have its own composition and recognizable Crystalize language;
applying facets to the original layout is a failure.

Use an organic, incomplete **crystal-cut boundary** built from varied facet
tips and short angular planes. Keep every transition into the paper crisp and
optically definite: no feathering, watercolor bleed, fog, or blurred alpha.
Surround it with generous negative space. The result must feel like an
editorial illustration, art print, postcard, or zine—not an altered photograph
and not an isolated object render.

### 5. Design the Paper Field

For landscape vignettes, default to warm ivory, cream, or quiet pale-neutral
paper with subtle grain. Keep it matte and substantially quieter than the
illustration. Use a dark field only when the user explicitly requests it or the
source cannot remain legible on a pale field. Never turn a pale landscape into
a floating crystal object merely to increase contrast.

### 6. Paint with Crystal

Apply material according to hierarchy, never because a region happens to appear in the source. Primary anchors may receive full crystalline treatment; secondary forms may be semi-crystalline, simplified, matte, atmospheric, or omitted; backgrounds should usually remain quiet.

Treat crystal as the **drawing medium inside the flat illustration**. Make its
glass clarity recognizable at first glance through variable transparency,
overlapping clear planes, internal refraction, transmitted light, selective
double images, and thin directional highlights. Preserve original colors and
use a natural hierarchy of irregular facet sizes: a few large structural
planes, varied medium facets, and smaller facets at focal details and optical
transitions. Add restrained thickness, bevels, caustics, dispersion, and tiny
precise facet shadows when they strengthen a tactile journal-like shallow
relief. The world should feel naturally made of living crystal, not covered by
a polygon filter. The work may remain 2D-first, but must not collapse into flat
animation, watercolor, gouache, opaque collage, low-poly rendering, or a 3D
crystal object. See `references/crystal-material.md` for execution.

Do not introduce major subjects absent from the source unless explicitly requested.

### 7. Identify and Title

Before naming the work, identify whether the primary anchor is a known
landmark, building, natural feature, or location. Check available metadata and,
when needed, verify a likely identity with reliable sources. Prefer the exact
landmark or location name when confidence is high. Never invent a place or
misclassify a structure; if identity remains uncertain, use a specific subject
name or derive **2–4 simple English words** from the scene's defining
relationship. Use Title Case, no punctuation, and no subtitle.

Set the title in a distinctive, highly legible classical handwritten script:
fine hairlines, shaded downstrokes, natural pressure variation, and restrained
custom flourishes. Avoid common wedding-script styling. Keep the title small
and clearly subordinate—approximately **40–55% of the vignette width** and no
more than **3–4% of canvas height**, unless text length requires less. Use soft
charcoal, gray-brown, or scene-derived muted ink. Leave generous space above
and below. Do not add signatures, logos, watermarks, decorative rules, or
additional copy. If the user supplies exact text, reproduce it verbatim.

## Final Quality Check

Verify: source orientation is preserved unless explicitly changed; 2–5 anchors
remain; the source is recognizable but compositionally reauthored; incidental
information is gone; the partial boundary and cream-paper negative space feel
intentional; crystal has natural transparency, varied facet scale, optical
depth, and immediately visible clarity; the perimeter is crisp; the work reads
neither as flat animation nor a filtered photo nor a 3D crystal object; and the
short title is semantically grounded, exact, legible, and subordinate.

Run the regression cases in `references/examples.md` when the source is visually complex.

## Core Formula

SOURCE PHOTO → EXTRACT 2–5 ANCHORS → SIMPLIFY INTO LAYERS → BUILD A PARTIAL
LANDSCAPE VIGNETTE → PLACE ON CREAM PAPER → PAINT WITH CRYSTALLINE PLANES →
ADD A 2–4 WORD TITLE → ART-DIRECT

> **Not a crystal filter. A new way of drawing.**
