# Offer Negotiation

A good negotiation ends with a package the user would have accepted anyway, agreed in writing, with the hiring manager still pleased they made the offer.

The offer conversation is the highest hourly-rate conversation of a career — a fifteen-minute exchange routinely moves five figures a year, and every subsequent raise and offer is anchored on the number it settles — and most people spend it apologising. Two failures do nearly all the damage. The first is accepting on the call, because the relief of finally having an offer is genuinely overwhelming and saying yes makes it stop; the number was drafted with negotiation expected, and accepting it immediately leaves the margin on the table permanently. The second is negotiating hard on base while ignoring the components that are worth more and are frequently easier for the company to move — level, sign-on, equity quantity and the terms attached to it. A third, quieter failure is negotiating well over a job the user has not actually evaluated, which is how someone ends up ten thousand better off and reporting to a manager they already had doubts about.

## Hard gates

- **Nothing is real until it is in writing.** A verbal offer is a plan. Do not resign, decline another process, or announce anything until a written offer carrying the agreed numbers is in hand.
- **Do not invent a competing offer or misstate current compensation.** Both are checkable — by a recruiter who knows the market, by a mutual contact, sometimes by a payslip request — and the negotiation is the first conversation of a working relationship you are about to depend on for years.
- **This is not tax, legal or financial advice.** Equity taxation, notice periods, and whether a non-compete is enforceable at all vary sharply by country and by state or province, and change. Where the sums or the clauses are material, say plainly that a professional in the user's jurisdiction should look before signing.
- **Do not fill a number the user has not given.** Bands, current salary and competing offers are the user's facts to supply. Where a figure is needed and absent, write `[TK: number]` and ask the specific question that fills it.

## Scope

Use for: deciding whether to accept, comparing offers, decomposing total compensation, understanding equity terms, planning and rehearsing the ask, levelling arguments, deadline handling, reviewing a written offer against what was said, handling a counteroffer, and declining.

Do not use for: writing a CV or preparing for interview rounds, promotion and performance cases inside a current employer, running a hiring loop from the employer's side, or setting compensation bands for a team.

## Workflow 1: Evaluate before you negotiate

Compensation is one column of the decision. Score the offer against priorities the user wrote down *before* the number arrived — priorities written afterwards are rationalisations, and the tell is that they always happen to favour the offer in hand.

Ask for the user's own weighting first. If they have none, propose these and have them adjust:

| Dimension | The question that actually reveals it |
| --- | --- |
| The work itself | What would you be doing in week six, concretely, and would you tell a friend about it |
| The manager | Have you seen them think under pressure, and what happened to the last person in this role |
| The team's trajectory | Is it growing, holding, or absorbing another team's leftovers |
| Company stage and runway | Months of runway, last raise, path to profitability, and who says so |
| Compensation | Total package, not base — Workflow 2 |
| Remote and travel | What is written down versus what the team actually does on Tuesdays |
| On-call load | Rotation size, pages last month, and whether it is compensated |
| Options in three years | Does this title, domain and company name open doors or narrow them |

Score each 1-5 against the weight, then apply two checks. What is the one thing that would make this a clear no at any salary — and is it present? And if the compensation were identical to the current role, would the user still move? A yes to the second means the negotiation is about getting paid properly for a decision already made, which is a much calmer conversation than one where the money is doing all the work.

## Workflow 2: Decompose the total compensation

Never compare offers on base. Build the whole picture, marking each component as cash the user can count on, cash they probably get, or a lottery ticket.

| Component | What to ask for, specifically |
| --- | --- |
| Base | The number, the band for the level, and where in the band the offer sits |
| Bonus | Target percentage, and what actually paid out the last two or three years across the company — target and actual are different numbers |
| Equity | The full term set below |
| Sign-on | Amount, payment schedule, and any clawback if the user leaves inside a year or two |
| Pension or retirement match | Match percentage and vesting of the employer contribution — often several thousand a year and easy to miss |
| Benefits that are real money | Health cover and its family cost, extra leave, learning budget, equipment, parental leave beyond statutory, visa or relocation support |

For equity, ask for all of it in writing, because a headline "$200k of equity" is uninterpretable without the terms: number of units or options rather than a currency figure, strike price where applicable, the current preferred and common (409A or equivalent) valuation, vesting schedule and cliff, whether there is a refresh policy and what recent refreshes looked like, what happens to unvested equity on leaving and what happens on acquisition or IPO, and the **post-termination exercise window** — the term that most often converts a paper fortune into nothing, because ninety days to fund an exercise plus its tax bill is a bill many people cannot pay.

Value private-company equity as a lottery ticket with a very wide distribution: the modal outcome across startups is zero, the tail is life-changing, and the company's own valuation is a negotiated number rather than a market price. A defensible practice is to compare offers on guaranteed cash first, then treat equity as a separate, explicitly labelled upside column — never to convert it to a single expected value and add it to base as if it were salary. Read `references/compensation-components.md` before advising on equity, bonus reality or benefits arithmetic.

## Workflow 3: Find the leverage you actually have

| Source | Strength | How to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A competing written offer | Strongest | Name that a decision is due, and what would make this one the choice. Do not demand a match; ask them to close a specific gap |
| An honest current-role alternative | Solid | "Staying is a real option; here is what would make moving obviously right" |
| Specificity about your value | Available to everyone | The two or three things in the JD you have already done, with evidence, tied to the ask |
| Timing — they are late in a long search | Real but unstated | Never mention it; it shows up as their flexibility |

Leverage you do not have cannot be manufactured. With no alternative and no scarce skill, the honest position is a well-reasoned ask made once, politely, with a reason — which still works more often than people expect, because a recruiter with band headroom would rather spend it than restart a search. Inventing a competing offer is a bad trade at any odds: it is checkable, the bluff is common enough that recruiters test it ("great, send the written offer and we will beat it"), and it poisons the relationship at the moment the user is about to depend on it.

## Workflow 4: The first number

- **Where salary history is unlawful to ask.** Several US states and cities, and a number of other jurisdictions, prohibit asking about salary history; some require a band on request or a posted range. This varies and changes, so check the user's jurisdiction rather than asserting a rule. Practically: the user can decline to give history in any jurisdiction — "I would rather anchor on the market rate for the level" — and asking for the band costs nothing anywhere.
- **Redirect to the band first.** "What is the range for this level?" is a normal question. Ask it before giving anything.
- **If pressed, give a considered range with a reason, not a single number.** A single number is a ceiling: it becomes the top of what they will offer, and if it lands low the offer arrives low and no argument recovers it. A range with a reason — levelling data, posted ranges for the level and city, what the user's scope has been — is defensible and moves the conversation to evidence.
- **Make the bottom of the range a number you would sign.** They will hear the bottom. Never state a range whose floor would disappoint.

## Workflow 5: Make one consolidated ask

Enthusiasm, then a specific ask, then a reason — in that order. Enthusiasm first because the recruiter's fear is wasting a week on someone who will not sign; specificity because "is there any flexibility?" invites the answer "no"; the reason because it lets the recruiter argue the case internally, which is who they actually have to persuade.

- **One ask, containing everything.** Base, level, sign-on, start date, remote terms — all in one message, prioritised. A drip of separate asks is the pattern that makes a hiring manager wonder whether the user will be like this about everything.
- **Ask the person for the thing they can change.** Recruiters usually have more discretion over sign-on and placement within a band than over base, and level changes almost always require the hiring manager plus a compensation partner. Ask who needs to approve which item; the answer tells you where the give is.
- **Then stop talking.** Silence after an ask is uncomfortable for about four seconds and is the cheapest tool available. Filling it is how people negotiate against themselves.

```text
I am glad we got here — this is the team I want to join, and the [specific
thing about the work] is exactly what I want to be doing next.

Before I sign, I would like to close the gap on a few things, in priority order:

1. Level. Based on [scope evidence], I believe this is a [target level] role.
2. Base. [Number], which reflects [band data / competing offer / current scope].
3. Sign-on of [number] to cover [unvested bonus / notice period / relocation].

If you can get to [the shape that closes it], I am ready to accept.
```

Say the last line only when it is true, because it converts the conversation from haggling into closing — and reneging on it costs the relationship.

## Workflow 6: Negotiate the level, not just the number

Levelling is the highest-value negotiation available. It moves base, bonus target, equity and the band you are next promoted from, all at once, and it compounds for years — a level down at the start is typically two to four years of catch-up, if it is ever caught up.

Make the case with evidence, not adjectives: the scope already owned (systems, headcount, budget, blast radius), decisions made without a safety net, and one artefact — a design, a migration, an incident review — that reads like the target level. Ask directly: "What separates [level] from [target level] here, and what would you need to see to place me at the higher one?" That question is answerable and often produces the criteria in writing.

Realistic when: the loop feedback was strong, the user is at the top of the lower band or already doing the higher level's work elsewhere, or the JD spans two levels. Not realistic when: the level was set before the loop and confirmed by a calibration panel, or the team has no headcount at the higher level. Ask about a re-levelling interview if one is offered, and weigh it honestly — it is a second chance, but it can also lower the offer already made.

## Workflow 7: Deadlines, the written offer, and resigning

Asking for time is normal and does not read as disinterest when it comes with warmth and a date: "I am excited about this and I want to give you a proper yes — can I come back to you by Thursday?" A week is unremarkable; longer needs a reason, such as a final round elsewhere, which can be said plainly.

An exploding offer — 24 or 48 hours, "this rate expires" — is information about the company more often than it is about the process. A firm no to any extension usually means either a rigid process or a deliberate attempt to prevent comparison, and both predict how the company will behave when the user later needs flexibility. Reasonable firms hold an offer for a week.

When the written offer arrives, read it against what was said, not against memory of the good feeling. Check that every negotiated item is present in the document — a verbally agreed sign-on that is not written down does not exist — and read: notice period on both sides, probation terms and what notice shrinks to during it, non-compete and non-solicit clauses (enforceability varies enormously by jurisdiction — in California, for instance, most non-competes are void, while other places enforce them; check locally rather than assuming), IP assignment and whether it reaches personal projects, sign-on clawback and its trigger period, bonus eligibility if the user leaves or is let go before payout, and any equity documents referenced but not attached, which should be requested. Resign only after that document is signed by both sides.

## Workflow 8: The counteroffer, and declining well

When the current employer counters, the money is real and the reasons for leaving usually are not addressed by it. Ask what changed: the counteroffer proves the budget existed before, and it was not offered. It also marks the user as a flight risk in the next planning round — quietly, and usually only visible later. Take one seriously only if the offer fixes the actual reason for leaving (scope, manager, work) rather than repricing it, and if the fix is written down with a date. If the reason was money alone, and the counteroffer meets it, accepting can be rational — say so honestly rather than reciting folklore, while naming the cost.

Decline the other offers by phone or a short note, quickly, with a real reason and no hedging: the market is small, recruiters and hiring managers move, and the person declined well this year runs the loop the user wants in three. Never leave a process without an answer.

## Standard output format: offer comparison

```text
## Priorities (written before the numbers)
[dimension] — weight [1-5] · [dimension] — weight [1-5] · ...
Walk-away condition: [the thing that makes this a no at any salary]

## Offer A: [company, title, level]
Guaranteed cash: base [ ] + sign-on [ ] (clawback: [ ])
Probable cash:   bonus target [ ]%, actual last 2 years [ ]
Upside:          [units] [type], strike [ ], vesting [ ]/[ ] cliff,
                 exercise window [ ], refresh [ ], acquisition terms [ ]
Real-money benefits: pension match [ ], leave [ ], other [ ]
Scores: work [ ] manager [ ] team [ ] stage [ ] remote [ ] on-call [ ] options [ ]

## Offer B: [as above]

## What the comparison actually turns on
[one or two sentences — usually not the base]

## The ask
Target: [level / base / sign-on], to: [recruiter or hiring manager]
Reason: [evidence]
Fallback that still closes it: [number or term]

## Open questions to send before deciding
- [question] — [what the answer changes]
```

## Anti-patterns

- **Accepting on the call.** The first number is drafted with room in it, and the relief of having an offer is the worst state in which to make the year's most expensive decision. "This is great news — can I read it properly and come back to you Thursday?" costs nothing and is expected.
- **Negotiating base and ignoring level.** A few thousand on base against a level that sets every future band, bonus target and equity refresh. The level is the compounding term; the base is one year.
- **Treating private equity as cash.** Adding a company's own valuation of its shares to base produces a total that cannot be spent, and it is how someone leaves a certain 20k on the table for an uncertain 60k.
- **Inventing a competing offer.** Checkable, frequently tested, and it starts the relationship with a lie that the recruiter may recall for years. If the leverage is not there, argue value instead.
- **Giving a single number when asked first.** It becomes the ceiling. A range with a reason keeps the conversation on evidence, and its floor is what they will hear.
- **A drip of separate asks.** Each one costs goodwill and re-opens a closed conversation; three in sequence reads as bad faith even when each is reasonable. Send them once, prioritised.
- **Resigning before the written offer.** Verbal offers are withdrawn — by a hiring freeze, a reorganisation, a background check, a changed mind — and the old job is already gone.
- **Taking the counteroffer.** It reprices the job without fixing it, proves the budget was always there, and quietly relabels the user as a flight risk. Accept one only when it addresses the actual reason for leaving, in writing.
