# Habit Change

A finished design is one written sentence naming the behaviour, the existing cue it follows, the version small enough to do on the worst day of the month, and the recovery rule — and it is working when the behaviour happened on at least five of the last seven days without the person having to decide each time.

Behaviour change fails in five reproducible ways, and none of them is insufficient willpower. The target is too big on day one, so the first bad day breaks it and the break is read as proof of a character defect. It is anchored to a clock time, so a late meeting or a bad night's sleep deletes the cue entirely. The environment is left as it was, so the wanted action keeps costing four steps and the unwanted one keeps costing none. Tracking becomes the project — a spreadsheet, an app, a streak counter — and the streak's first break takes the habit down with it. And the person is running on the motivation that made them start, which is a depleting resource, without ever converting the behaviour into something the context triggers on its own. This skill fixes each one mechanically: one behaviour, shrunk; an existing cue rather than a time; friction moved before effort is applied; a recovery rule written on day one, in advance of the day it is needed; and a review that asks whether the behaviour has become automatic rather than whether the streak is intact.

## Scope

Use for: starting a behaviour, stopping one, restarting after it collapsed, choosing a cue, redesigning the environment around a behaviour, deciding what to track, and diagnosing why the last three attempts died.

Do not use for: what to eat, calorie and macro targets, or meal planning — that is `health-coach`, including the nutrition half of "I want to get healthier". Do not use it for the weekly planning and commitment cycle, capacity or a Friday reset, which is `weekly-review`. Money routines and budgeting belong in `personal-finance`; renewals, forms and recurring admin belong in `life-admin`.

"I want to exercise more" sits on the line and splits cleanly: the frequency, the cue, the smallest version and the recovery rule are this skill; what to eat around the training is `health-coach`; slotting three sessions into next week's real calendar is `weekly-review`. Where a request is mostly one of the other two, hand it over by name rather than running this procedure on it.

Weight loss splits on the same line, and it is worth stating because the request usually arrives named after the outcome. "I want to lose weight — help me build a habit of going to the gym three times a week" is this skill: what is being asked for is a behaviour, and the cue it follows, the version that survives a bad day and the recovery rule are what decide whether it happens. The calorie target, what to eat around the training, and whether the plan moves the weight at all are `health-coach`. Design the behaviour here and hand the eating half over by name.

## What the evidence actually supports

Quote these numbers rather than the folklore, and quote the spread as well as the middle.

| Finding | The number | What it means for the design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to automaticity | Lally and colleagues (2010) tracked 96 people and found a median of 66 days, with a range from 18 to 254 | Set the review at day 66, not day 21, and tell the person the variance up front so a slow build is not read as failure |
| Implementation intentions | Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis (2006, 94 studies) found a medium-to-large effect, d around 0.65, for plans specifying when and where | Write the cue and place into the sentence; an intention without a location is a wish |
| Context does the work | Wood, Quinn and Kashy (2002) found roughly 43% of daily behaviour was repeated in the same physical context | Change the context and you change the behaviour more cheaply than by deciding harder |
| Friction is measured in inches | Rozin and colleagues (2011) cut how often people took a food item by moving it about 25 cm further away | An extra step is a real intervention, not a metaphor |
| One miss is survivable | In Lally's data a single missed day did not measurably dent the automaticity curve | Build the plan around never missing twice, not around never missing |
| Lapses collapse when read as failure | Marlatt and Gordon's relapse-prevention work (1985) named the abstinence violation effect: the lapse is small, the self-judgement after it is what ends the attempt | The recovery rule is written before the lapse precisely so the interpretation is decided in advance |

The twenty-one-day figure has no evidential basis; it is a misreading of Maxwell Maltz's 1960 observation about how long his surgical patients took to adjust to a new face. Say so when someone quotes it, because expecting automaticity at three weeks and not getting it is itself a common quitting point.

## Workflow

### 1. Pick one behaviour and make it a sentence

One. A person attempting three changes at once is running three untested designs against the same finite attention, and when it collapses they cannot tell which one was wrong. Ask what they would keep if only one survived, and park the rest in writing so parking does not feel like losing them.

Then convert the goal into a behaviour. "Get fitter" is an outcome and cannot be performed on Tuesday; "walk to the end of the road and back after I put the kettle on" can. Outcomes belong in the review, not in the plan.

### 2. Shrink it until a bad day cannot stop it

Set the size against the worst realistic day in a month — ill, travelling, a bad night with a child, a late finish — not against the day the person is planning on. A design that only works on good days will run for as long as the good days last, which is rarely more than a week or two.

| The stated ambition | The version that survives a bad day |
| --- | --- |
| Run 5 km four times a week | Put the shoes on and go out of the door; the run is optional once outside |
| Read 30 pages a night | One page, in the chair, before the phone comes out |
| Meditate 20 minutes | Three breaths after sitting down at the desk |
| Write 1000 words | Open the file and write one sentence |

The small version is the floor, not the target. On a normal day the person does more and should; the floor exists so that the streak is broken by genuine impossibility rather than by an ordinary Tuesday. Ask directly: could you do this with a fever and four hours' sleep? If not, halve it again.

### 3. Anchor it to an existing cue, not a time of day

A clock time is a weak cue because it competes with everything else on the clock. An existing, already-automatic behaviour is a strong cue because it fires on its own and drags the new behaviour behind it. Write it as: **after I [existing behaviour], I will [new behaviour] in [place]**.

Good anchors happen daily without being remembered, end cleanly rather than trailing off, and occur in the place where the new behaviour can be done: boiling the kettle, closing the laptop lid, locking the front door, putting the youngest to bed, sitting down at the desk. Bad anchors are things the person already forgets, things that happen at wildly variable times, or things that happen somewhere else — "after lunch" is not an anchor, "after I rinse my lunch plate" is.

Where the behaviour genuinely has no daily anchor, use the strongest available recurring event and accept a weaker design: a calendar block is the last resort, not the first choice, and it needs the environment work in step 4 to compensate.

### 4. Design the environment before applying effort

Willpower is a poor daily input and an excellent one-off input. Spend it once, on the layout, and the layout pays out every day afterwards. The rule in both directions: make the wanted behaviour the path of least resistance, and put at least one real step in front of the unwanted one.

| Direction | Mechanism | Concrete |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toward the wanted behaviour | Remove steps and decisions from the start | Kit laid out the night before; the book on the pillow; the guitar on a stand rather than in a case; the first exercise chosen in advance so nothing is decided while standing in the hall |
| Toward the wanted behaviour | Attach it to something already wanted | Temptation bundling, tested by Milkman, Minson and Volpp (2014): the audiobook is only allowed at the gym |
| Away from the unwanted behaviour | Add steps, delay, or a second person | Phone charging in another room overnight; apps logged out so each use needs a password; the snacks bought in single units at the shop rather than in a box at home |
| Away from the unwanted behaviour | Remove the cue rather than resist it | Notifications off at the operating-system level; the app deleted and used through a browser; the route home changed so it does not pass the shop |

Prefer a change that holds while the person is tired and unmotivated over one that requires them to notice and decide. An extra step that can be undone in three seconds still works, because the point is not to make the behaviour impossible but to make it deliberate.

### 5. Write the recovery rule now, not on the day

This is the clause that separates a design that survives from one that does not, and it has to be written before the first miss, because on the day of the miss the person is choosing while feeling like a failure.

Write it in this shape, in the person's own words:

- **Never twice.** One missed day is data; two consecutive missed days is the pattern that precedes abandonment, so the second day is the one with the hard rule attached. On the day after a miss, the floor version is non-negotiable and the full version is off the table.
- **What counts as a miss.** Decide in advance whether a shortened version counts as done. It should: a floor day is a success, and counting it as a failure is how the abstinence violation effect gets in.
- **The restart procedure.** After a break of a week or more, do not resume at the old size. Restart at the floor for a week, and treat the break as a design fault to be diagnosed in step 7 rather than a moral event.
- **The sentence to say.** One line, decided now, for the moment after a miss — something closer to "that was one day, tomorrow is the floor version" than to anything about discipline.

### 6. Track the minimum that proves it happened

Most tracking fails because it becomes a second habit that also needs forming, and because it measures the streak rather than the behaviour. Track one binary fact per day — did the floor version happen — recorded in under five seconds, in the place the behaviour happens. A wall calendar and a pen beats an app for most people, because the app needs unlocking a phone, which is the same phone the environment work in step 4 is trying to keep out of reach.

Do not track quantity in the first month. Quantity invites optimisation, optimisation raises the floor, and a raised floor is what breaks on the first bad day. Do not track more than one habit on the same sheet at the start; one broken row makes the others feel broken too.

The streak is a motivator with a defect: its value is destroyed by exactly the event the design expects. If the person is streak-motivated, count something that a miss does not zero — total days done this month, or weeks in which the floor was hit five times or more. Both keep rewarding a recovered week.

### 7. Review at day 14, 30 and 66

Three reviews, each asking a different question, which takes about five minutes each.

| Review | The question | The action it can produce |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day 14 | Is the cue firing — do you find yourself starting without having thought about it? | Change the anchor if the answer is no; keep the size fixed |
| Day 30 | Where do the misses cluster — which day, which place, which preceding event? | Fix that specific situation; misses are almost never evenly spread |
| Day 66 | Does it still require a decision? How hard would skipping it feel? | Raise the floor by a small increment, or add a second habit — but only one of the two |

Read the misses as design evidence. A behaviour that fails on Thursdays has a Thursday problem, not a motivation problem, and the fix is usually a different anchor for Thursdays rather than more resolve.

## Stopping something works differently

Starting adds a behaviour to an empty slot; stopping leaves a slot that the old behaviour will reclaim unless something else takes it. Four differences change the design.

**There is no floor version.** "Smoke less" and "scroll a bit" are not floors, they are the behaviour at reduced volume with the cue intact. For a nicotine-type behaviour — a specific cue, a supply you control, no physical dependence that stopping would destabilise — abrupt cessation beats tapering: Lindson-Hawley and colleagues (2016), in a randomised trial of nearly 700 smokers, found abrupt quitting produced higher abstinence at four weeks than gradual reduction, 49% against 39%. Set a date rather than a slope. The evidence is a smoking trial and the rule stops at that shape. Withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines and some other medications is a medical event that abrupt cessation can make dangerous, so anything in that group goes to a clinician for a supervised plan before any date is set.

**Suppression rebounds.** Wegner, Schneider, Carter and White (1987) are the reason "just don't think about it" fails: people told not to think of a white bear thought of it more, during the instruction and afterwards. Wegner's ironic process theory (1994) supplied the mechanism — suppression requires a monitor that searches for the thought, and the search is what keeps producing it. Design around the cue instead of around the urge — remove the trigger, leave the room, change the route — and give the slot a replacement behaviour that uses the same hands and the same two minutes.

**Name the trigger, not the habit.** Log the next ten instances with what preceded each: time, place, who was present, what feeling. The list is almost always short and situational — the after-dinner cigarette, the phone at the first boring moment of a meeting, the drink at the point of putting the children to bed. Each entry gets its own environment fix, and the ones with no fix get a substitute.

**The recovery rule is more important, not less.** A single lapse in a stopping attempt is the classic abstinence violation point. Write the rule as: a lapse is one event, it does not restart a counter, and the next instance of the trigger has a named alternative. If the behaviour is alcohol, nicotine, drugs, gambling or self-harm, say plainly that this skill is a design tool and not treatment, and point at a clinician or a specialist service rather than running this design on top of it.

## Telling a formed habit from one running on motivation

These are different states with different failure risks, and the difference is not how long it has been going.

| Signal | Still running on motivation | Becoming automatic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting | The person decides each time, often by negotiating | It starts on the cue, and skipping requires an active decision |
| A disrupted week | The behaviour stops and does not resume without a fresh push | It resumes when the ordinary week resumes |
| Feeling | Doing it feels like an achievement | Doing it feels unremarkable; not doing it feels slightly wrong |
| Tracking | The tracker is what makes it happen | The tracker could be dropped and the behaviour would continue |
| Language | "I am trying to run" | "I run on Tuesdays" |

If the design is still in the left column at day 66, that is not a failure, given Lally's range ran to 254 days — but treat it as a signal to examine the cue and the size rather than to add effort. Motivation-dependent habits die during holidays, illness and house moves, so a person in the left column going into a disruption needs the floor version and the recovery rule restated before it, not after.

## Output format

Produce this, filled in, and nothing longer. It is meant to be readable in twenty seconds on a bad day.

```markdown
## Habit: [the behaviour in four words]

**The sentence:** After I [existing cue], I will [floor version] in [place].

**Floor version:** [the version that survives a fever and four hours' sleep]
**Normal version:** [what a good day looks like — optional, never the target]

### Environment
- Toward it: [steps removed, things laid out, decisions pre-made]
- Away from the alternative: [the extra step, the removed cue]

### Recovery rule
- A miss is: [definition — the floor version counts as done]
- Never twice: [what happens on the day after a miss]
- After a week off: [restart at the floor for seven days]
- The sentence I say: [one line, written now]

### Tracking
[The one binary fact, where it is recorded, in under five seconds]

### Reviews
- Day 14 — is the cue firing on its own?
- Day 30 — where do the misses cluster?
- Day 66 — does it still take a decision?

### Parked for now
- [the other changes not being attempted yet]
```

## Anti-patterns

**The January stack.** Four changes started on the same Monday. They compete for the same attention, they fail together, and the failure teaches nothing because no single variable was under test. Run one, add the second only after the first has survived a bad week.

**Anchoring to a clock.** "Gym at 7am" survives until the first late night, and then the cue is simply gone with nothing to bring it back. An anchor that is itself automatic — the kettle, the laptop lid, the front door — fires whether or not the person remembers.

**The heroic first week.** Starting at the target size produces seven excellent days, a plateau of soreness or boredom, and abandonment inside a fortnight, with a memory that says "I tried this and could not sustain it". Start below what feels worth doing; the embarrassment of the small version is the point.

**Willpower as the daily input.** Deciding again every day costs something each time and fails exactly on the days when things are hardest. Spend the effort once on the layout, where it keeps paying out.

**Streak worship.** A counter that resets to zero converts one ordinary missed day into a reason to stop, which is the abstinence violation effect with a user interface. Count days done this month instead, or weeks that hit the floor five times.

**Tracking as the project.** A spreadsheet with weight, duration, mood and a weekly chart is a second habit that also needs forming, and it will fail first and take the real one with it. One binary mark, five seconds, at the site of the behaviour.

**No recovery rule.** Without one written in advance, the decision after the first miss is made while feeling like a failure, which reliably produces the second miss. Write it on day one, when it is a cheap sentence rather than an expensive one.

**Reducing instead of stopping.** "Fewer cigarettes", "less scrolling" keeps the cue, the supply and the ritual intact and relies on judgement at the exact moment judgement is worst. Set a stop date and give the slot a replacement.

**Treating misses as a character verdict.** A missed Thursday is evidence about Thursdays: what happened before it, where the person was, what the cue collided with. Diagnose the situation and change the design; resolve is not a lever you can pull twice.

**Quoting twenty-one days.** It sets an expectation the evidence does not support, and the disappointment at day twenty-two is itself a common quitting point. Use 66 days as the median and name the 18-to-254 range out loud.
