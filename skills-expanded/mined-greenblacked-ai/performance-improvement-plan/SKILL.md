# Performance Improvement Plan

A formal plan is done well when the person can state, without reading it, exactly what is expected, how it will be measured, what support they will get, and when it ends — and when the outcome at the end is genuinely open. The plan is a real attempt to keep someone, with a stated exit if it fails, or it should not be written at all.

The job is hard because the two ways to run this process are opposite and both are common. One is a genuine, supported, time-boxed attempt to change behaviour, run the same way you would want it run for you. The other is documentation assembled to justify a decision already taken, where the plan is a formality on the way to an exit and everyone in the room knows it. The second is the one that produces the employment claim, because the record shows the reasoning was written after the conclusion. This skill exists to hold the first shape: specific gaps, real support, honest measurement, and an outcome that the evidence decides rather than the manager. It also exists to stop the plan being the first time the person hears there is a problem — that failure belongs one step back, to the informal conversation, and a plan that opens with a surprise is already compromised.

## Scope

Use for: deciding whether a formal plan is the right next step; writing the plan and its measures; running the improvement period and its check-ins; the end-of-period decision and what follows; the record that documents it, kept properly.

Do not use for: the first, informal conversation that should come before any of this, or delivering the end-of-period decision as a hard conversation (both are `difficult-conversation`); the written review, the rating or a promotion case (`growth-review`); or the decision to hire or not (`hiring-loop`).

## Non-negotiables

**The outcome is open until the evidence closes it.** If the decision is already made, say so plainly and run it as an exit with notice, not as a plan with a predetermined result. A plan run towards a fixed conclusion is the specific artefact that makes a defensible people decision indefensible.

**No new issues once the period starts.** The gaps are the ones named on day one. A concern raised for the first time in week six of a plan is evidence the plan was miswritten, and it is the most common way the process becomes unfair.

**The support is real.** A plan that lists expectations and no support is a trap, not a plan. Name what the manager, the team and the company will provide, and provide it.

**Partner with HR or the equivalent from day one**, and follow the local process. Employment law varies by jurisdiction and this skill is not a substitute for advice from someone who knows yours.

**The person has heard all of it before.** Every gap in the written plan should be traceable to an earlier conversation. The plan formalises what was already said; it does not introduce it.

## Workflow

### 1. Decide whether a plan is the right tool

Before writing anything, name which of four things this is, because they need different actions.

| Situation | The right move |
| --- | --- |
| A capable person in the wrong role | Move them, with a plan to find the fit — not a performance plan |
| A capable person whose expectations were never set or supported | Fix the management failure first, then judge; a plan here is unfair |
| A person genuinely below the standard after clear feedback and support | A formal plan, run honestly |
| A decision already made to exit | An exit with notice and a fair process, not a plan dressed up as one |

Ask what changed since the last conversation. If nothing changed in the work but a decision has appeared, you are in the last row, and the honest version is the exit.

### 2. Check your own contribution first

The same self-check `onboarding-plan` uses applies here, and it belongs in the written record whichever way the plan goes.

- Were the expectations written down and shared, or assumed?
- Was the work the right size for the level, or set up to fail?
- Did the person get specific feedback, with dates, before now?
- Was the support offered actually delivered?
- Is anything here a mismatch between the person and the role rather than a shortfall?

Where any of the first four answers is no, or the last answer is yes, address the gap and restart the clock on the judgement. That is accurate, fair, and part of what has to change.

### 3. State the gaps as observable behaviour

Each gap is behaviour, then impact, then the evidence — the unit `growth-review` uses, for the same reason: a claim the person cannot act on is not a plan item.

| Not this | This |
| --- | --- |
| "Needs to be more senior" | "Three design reviews in Q2 shipped without the dependent teams being consulted; two of them reworked their plans afterwards" |
| "Poor communication" | "Two incidents were escalated after the agreed window; the on-call runbook says the window is thirty minutes" |
| "Not delivering" | "Two of five committed items landed in the quarter; the estimates were revised upward after the mid-quarter check-in" |

Two or three gaps, not ten. A plan with ten items is a wish list, and it signals that the real issue is something else.

### 4. Write the plan

Every plan has the same eight parts. `references/plan-structure.md` has the template and worked examples of each.

1. **The gaps**, in the behaviour-impact-evidence form above.
2. **The expected behaviour**, stated so the person knows what success looks like specifically, not in the abstract.
3. **The measures** — observable, and within the person's control. "Delivers the two committed items with a mid-point check" is a measure; "shows more ownership" is not.
4. **The support**: the manager's time, pairing, mentoring, training, a reduced load, clearer context. Be concrete about who provides what.
5. **The check-in cadence** — weekly is usual, with a written note after each.
6. **The start and end dates.** A plan has a fixed length, typically one to three months, set at the start. An open-ended plan is indefinite pressure and usually a legal problem.
7. **The review point and the possible outcomes**, stated up front: meeting the expectations, partial improvement, or not meeting them, and what each leads to.
8. **The signature and the date.** The person receives a copy and has a chance to respond in writing.

### 5. Run the period honestly

The plan is only as good as the way it is run.

- **Hold the check-ins**, at the agreed cadence, even when nothing has changed — especially then.
- **Write a short note after each one**, same day: what was discussed, what the person did well, what still needs to change, the date. Contemporaneous notes are the record; a summary written at the end is not.
- **Measure against the stated measures only.** Do not add a new concern to the list because it surfaced.
- **Apply the same standard** you would to anyone else with the same behaviour.
- **Give credit visibly.** If the person is meeting the expectations, say so in the check-in and in the record, so the end-of-period decision rests on evidence rather than on the momentum of having started a plan.

### 6. Reach the decision on the evidence

At the end date, there are three outcomes, and the plan said what each leads to.

- **Met.** Close the plan in writing, say so, and then do the harder part: the gap existed for a reason, and the reason has not gone away. Keep the support that worked and the check-ins that mattered.
- **Partly met.** Either extend once, in writing, with a stated reason and a new end date — or close it as met-with-support and keep the support. Extending twice is a decision to exit that nobody has said out loud.
- **Not met.** The exit conversation is a hard conversation, and the delivery is `difficult-conversation`; this skill has produced the record and the decision it rests on. Follow the local process for notice, severance and the handover.

### 7. Handle the team aftermath

The rest of the team watches how this is run, and draws a conclusion about how it would be run for them. Share only neutral operational changes that affect their work, such as coverage, handover and ownership. Keep the plan's existence, support, assessment and outcome within the approved need-to-know group; agree any communication with HR. Redistribute the work explicitly, and check that nobody has concluded that a bad quarter puts their job at risk.

## Output format

```markdown
## Performance improvement plan — [name], [role], [level]

### The gaps
[Per gap: behaviour, impact, evidence. Two or three. Each traceable to an earlier
conversation, with the date.]

### Expected behaviour
[What success looks like, specifically, in each of those areas.]

### Measures
[Observable, within the person's control, with how each is checked and how often.]

### Support
[Who provides what: manager time, pairing, mentoring, training, a reduced load, clearer
context. Concrete names and hours.]

### Check-ins
[Cadence, owner, and that a written note follows each.]

### Period
[Start date, end date, and the review point.]

### Outcomes at the review
[What meeting, partly meeting and not meeting the expectations each lead to.]

### Sign-off
[Date the person received it, and their right to respond in writing.]
```

## Anti-patterns

**The pre-decided plan.** The outcome is set before the period starts, and the plan is the paperwork. It fools nobody, least of all a tribunal, and the dates and tone give it away. If the decision is made, run an exit.

**Vague goals.** "Improve communication" cannot be started on Monday and cannot be measured on Friday, so the person fails a standard they were never shown. Every gap is a behaviour with an example.

**No support.** Expectations with nothing offered is a trap, and it reads as one. Name the support and deliver it.

**A surprise first mention.** The plan is the first time the person hears there is a problem, which means the earlier conversation did not happen or did not land. Go back a step.

**Moving goalposts.** A new concern raised mid-period, or a measure tightened after the fact. The gaps are fixed on day one; anything else is a different process.

**Extending indefinitely.** One extension with a written reason is fair. Two is an exit nobody has said out loud, and the person is the last to know.

**Running it alone.** No HR, no written process, no contemporaneous notes, until the decision is challenged and the record turns out to be a reconstruction. Involve HR from day one and write the notes the week they happen.

**Using the plan to avoid a harder conversation.** A layoff, a redundancy or a role change dressed up as performance is unfair to the person and a risk to the company. Name what it actually is.
