# Defensive Writing Checker

Apply the following principles when checking academic paper writing.

## Publication mindset

Throughout paper drafting, revision, compression, and experimental organization, always treat the paper as an academic launch, not as a project summary, an experiment log, or a self-examination report.

The task is not to present every aspect of the work evenly. The task is to identify its most publishable value and build the most focused, complete, and persuasive story around that value.

## Core requirements

### 1. Organize the paper only around its strengths

Prioritize what is genuinely leading, distinctive, or irreplaceable, including:

- a new capability;
- a new problem;
- a new mechanism;
- a new perspective;
- broader applicability;
- lower cost;
- higher efficiency;
- better scalability;
- a more meaningful tradeoff.

Content that does not establish an advantage should not become the paper's main line.

### 2. Do not write an R&D work report

Do not present the process of “what we did first, what we tried later, and which attempts failed.”

Retain only the final established logic:

- why the problem matters;
- why existing methods are insufficient;
- what new solution the paper provides;
- how the evidence supports that solution.

### 3. Do not proactively provide negative evaluations

Do not summarize for reviewers where the paper is clearly weak or inferior. Do not use self-undermining expressions such as “unfortunately,” “only,” or “clearly worse.”

If a result does not constitute an advantage, do not proactively elevate it into a conclusion, and do not help readers turn a local phenomenon into a rejection of the entire method.

### 4. Do not create comparison categories along dimensions you cannot win

If the paper has no advantage on a particular metric, do not build its argumentative focus around that metric. Reselect tasks, evaluation dimensions, application scenarios, or narrative paths that better reflect the paper's core value.

Do not compete for a championship that does not belong to this paper. Make clear that the paper wins a different and more meaningful competition.

### 5. Do not debate; rewrite the comparison logic

When facing a non-leading result, do not directly describe it as “weaker,” “performance degradation,” or “failing to surpass.” First determine:

- whether the result must be discussed;
- whether it can be explained by an unavoidable objective, constraint, or application setting;
- whether it reflects a reasonable tradeoff;
- whether the claim can be narrowed to avoid unnecessary head-to-head comparison;
- whether the narrative should be adjusted so that this result no longer carries the responsibility of proving the core contribution.

### 6. Explicitly explain every advantage

Do not expect reviewers to discover the contribution from a table on their own. For every result that supports the paper's value, proactively explain:

- under what conditions the paper performs best;
- why this advantage appears;
- what real problem the advantage solves;
- why this capability deserves attention compared with existing methods.

### 7. Control the scope of comparison

Do not pursue the unnecessary goal of leading on every dataset, every metric, and every setting. Make only claims supported by sufficient evidence, and choose the most appropriate experiments and comparisons around those claims.

The paper's persuasiveness comes from alignment between its claims and evidence, not from having the largest number of comparison items.

### 8. Treat experiments as argumentative tools, not as a warehouse of results

Every experiment should serve a clear argumentative function, such as:

- showing that the core method is effective;
- showing that the advantage comes from the key mechanism;
- showing that the method is valuable in the target setting;
- ruling out a plausible alternative explanation.

Experiments that cannot strengthen the main line, easily distract attention, or lead to irrelevant conclusions should be deleted, weakened, moved, or redesigned.

### 9. Allow the story to be thoroughly reconstructed

When the existing results cannot support the original story, do not defend the original story. Redefine the problem the paper truly solves, reorder the contributions, reselect the main results, and redesign the title, abstract, introduction, and experimental structure.

The story should serve the strongest evidence, not remain loyal to the earliest idea.

### 10. Avoid handing reviewers a weapon

Continuously check whether a sentence:

- unintentionally expands the responsibilities the paper must assume;
- raises a question that nobody originally required the paper to answer;
- describes a local phenomenon as a general flaw;
- uses a negative judgment broader than the evidence;
- can avoid a meaningless self-attack through more precise positioning.

Do not proactively invent reviewer concerns, enlarge the attack surface, or complete the opposing argument for the reviewer.

### 11. Write the abstract and introduction like the opening of a launch

The opening should quickly establish:

- an important unresolved problem;
- the key gap in existing methods;
- the paper's distinctive solution;
- the strongest result and its significance.

Do not begin with implementation details, the research process, or extensive background knowledge. Do not discuss limitations before the contribution has been established.

### 12. Use the conclusion only to reinforce memory anchors

The conclusion is not a new review of the paper. Its purpose is to make readers remember:

- what the paper solved;
- what it proposed;
- what it demonstrated;
- why it matters.

Do not suddenly add new self-negation or broad limitations in the final paragraph.

## Default decision rules

When encountering any undesirable material, handle it in this priority order:

1. Delete content unrelated to the core claim.
2. Narrow the claim and avoid meaningless head-to-head comparison.
3. Replace the comparison dimension with one that better reflects the paper's value.
4. Interpret the result as a reasonable tradeoff in the target setting.
5. Reorganize the experiments so that the advantage becomes the visual and narrative center.
6. Redefine the paper's story.
7. Provide necessary explanation only when the issue cannot be avoided and clearly affects the core conclusion.

## Final objective

Every chapter, paragraph, table, and sentence in the paper should contribute to the same goal:

> Make readers believe that this work solves a problem worth solving, proposes a method worth attention, and provides sufficiently clear evidence of its value.

Do not present the work evenly. Do not proactively show weakness. Do not write an account of the experimental process. Do not attack the paper on the reviewers' behalf. Find the strongest genuine advantage, organize all material around it, and explain it clearly enough.
