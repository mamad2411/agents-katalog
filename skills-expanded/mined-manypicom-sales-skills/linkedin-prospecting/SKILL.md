# LinkedIn Prospecting

LinkedIn is the best source of current job titles and the worst channel to automate aggressively. Both facts follow from the same thing: it's a walled platform that actively defends itself, and account restrictions are permanent enough to matter.

Used as research plus a light touch alongside email, it lifts reply rates meaningfully. Used as an automation channel at volume, it gets accounts restricted and the sender learns nothing.

## What it's genuinely best at

**Current titles.** Every other source decays; LinkedIn is updated by the person themselves. For confirming you have the right person in the right role right now, nothing else comes close.

**Finding the right person.** Filtering a company's employees by function and seniority is faster and more accurate than guessing from a website.

**Job change detection.** Someone leaving is a dead lead at the old company and a warm one at the new. Someone new in a buying role is one of the strongest triggers there is. See `funding-news-signals`.

**Research material.** Recent posts, comments, shared articles and profile changes are dated, public and specific — good material for a first line, if used carefully.

**Warm paths.** Shared connections, groups, alumni. A genuine mutual connection changes everything about a first message.

## The limits, and they're real

Approximate daily ceilings for a normal account. They vary by account age, standing and whether you're on a paid tier, and the platform doesn't publish them.

| Action | Rough safe ceiling |
|---|---|
| Connection requests | 15-20/day, 100/week |
| Profile views | 100-150/day |
| Messages to connections | 30-50/day |
| InMail | Per your plan's allowance |
| Follows | 30-50/day |

Exceeding them produces warnings, then temporary restrictions, then permanent ones. A restricted account loses the network it took years to build, and appeals mostly fail.

**Connection acceptance rate matters more than volume.** A low acceptance rate signals spraying, and the platform notices. Twenty targeted requests at 40% acceptance is better in every way than a hundred at 8%.

## On automation tools

Be straight with users about this rather than either promoting or refusing.

- **Automating LinkedIn violates the user agreement.** Not criminal, but the platform enforces it and the penalty is the account.
- **Browser-extension tools** operating in your session are harder to detect than cloud tools logging in from a datacentre IP, which are detected routinely.
- **Detection has improved and continues to.** Tools that were safe last year aren't now.
- **The cost is asymmetric.** Modest efficiency gain against losing an account you can't rebuild.

The defensible middle: use LinkedIn manually for research and a small number of genuine touches, and run volume through email where you own the infrastructure. If someone insists on automation, keep it well under the manual limits, use a browser-based tool, warm the account slowly, and accept the risk knowingly.

## The multichannel sequence

The pattern that works, and it's mostly email with LinkedIn as reinforcement.

```
Day 0    Email 1                      The pitch, personalised
Day 1    LinkedIn: view profile       Passive. They often see it
Day 3    Email 2                      New angle
Day 5    LinkedIn: connection request No note, or one short line
Day 7    Email 3                      Value, no ask
Day 10   LinkedIn: message            Only if they connected
Day 14   Email 4                      Close-out
```

Why this order: email does the work, LinkedIn provides recognition. Someone who's seen your name three times in different places responds differently from someone seeing a cold email once.

**Never send the same message on both channels.** It reads as automated because it is.

**Stop everything on a reply from either channel.** Continuing on LinkedIn after they replied to your email is the most common multichannel failure and it undoes the goodwill immediately.

## Connection requests

With a note or without? Both work and the data is genuinely mixed.

- **No note** often has a higher acceptance rate. Low friction, nothing to object to.
- **A short note** produces lower acceptance but better-qualified acceptance.

If you write one, keep it under 200 characters and make it a reason, not a pitch:

> Saw your post on outbound routing — we hit exactly that problem last year. Following your work.

What doesn't work: pitching in the connection request. It's the fastest route to being ignored and reported, and it makes the acceptance useless anyway because they now expect a sales message.

## Messaging connections

Once connected, the message is a cold email with a shorter format. Same rules: specific opener, one small ask, no wall of text, no pitch deck link.

> Thanks for connecting. Quick one — you mentioned in your post that the SDR ramp was the bottleneck. Is that still the case, or did you solve it?

Ask about their situation rather than describing yours. A question about them gets answered; a paragraph about you doesn't.

## Research without automation

Manual research is slow but it's the tier-1 play for your best leads. What's worth reading:

- **Recent posts and comments.** Dated, public, specific. The best first-line material on the platform.
- **The about section**, where people describe what they own in their own words.
- **Tenure.** Someone three months into a role is a very different prospect from someone at eight years.
- **Previous companies.** A path from a company that used your product is a warm angle.
- **What they engage with**, which tells you what they care about right now.

Referencing a post is strong. Referencing something private, or something that requires having studied their profile closely, tips into uncomfortable. The test: would they be pleased or unsettled that you knew this? Public post, pleased. Detailed career history recited back at them, unsettled.

## Getting emails from LinkedIn identification

The common workflow: identify the right person on LinkedIn, then find their work email elsewhere and run the sequence over email where you control the infrastructure.

That's a sound approach, with one requirement — the address still has to be verified before sending. Pattern-inferred addresses from a name and a domain bounce at rates that damage domains. See `email-verification`.

## Don't do these

- **Don't automate at volume.** The downside is permanent.
- **Don't pitch in a connection request.**
- **Don't send identical content on both channels.**
- **Don't message everyone who accepts.** Some connections are worth having and not selling to.
- **Don't scrape profile data** in ways the user agreement prohibits, and be honest with clients about that exposure.
- **Don't treat a profile view as a touch worth counting.**
- **Don't continue on LinkedIn after an email reply.**
- **Don't build the programme on LinkedIn.** You don't own the channel and it can be taken away.
