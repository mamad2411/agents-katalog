# Serial Position + Peak-End Rule + Zeigarnik Effect

Sequence and completion group: how position and closure shape memory and motivation.

## Serial Position Effect
Items at the start (primacy) and end (recency) of a list are recalled far better than the middle. Put the most important options first or last in a list/menu — not buried in the middle. Applies to nav menus, pricing tiers, form field order.

## Peak-End Rule
Users judge an entire experience mostly by its most intense moment (peak, positive or negative) and by how it ends — not by the average of every step. Invest disproportionately in: (1) removing or softening the single worst moment in a flow (e.g., a confusing error, a slow step), and (2) making the final screen/confirmation feel good (clear success state, small delight) since it's what's remembered afterward.

## Zeigarnik Effect
Incomplete tasks stick in memory and create tension until resolved, more than completed ones. Use progress indicators and "you're 80% done" framing to leverage this for onboarding/profile completion. Also explains why abandoned carts/drafts are effective to remind users about — the incompleteness itself is the hook. Don't overuse for manipulative pressure (fake scarcity, guilt-based nagging).

## Review checklist
1. Are the most important list items placed first/last, not mid-list?
2. What's the worst single moment in this flow, and can it be softened?
3. Does the flow end on a positive, clear note?
4. Is there a legitimate (non-manipulative) use of incompleteness to drive completion?
