# Industry Objection Playbook

### When to use
A prospect says: "You've never worked in our industry before." You're trying to enter a new vertical but worry you'll be perceived as an outsider. A competitor points out you lack "industry experience." You're a consultant with proven chops in one industry trying to apply them to another. You want to overcome the "you need to be from our world" objection without giving up.

### The framework
1. **Reframe industry experience as a potential liability.** Industries often hide bad practices because "that's how we do it here." Ask: What are the worst practices in your industry that insiders assume are unavoidable? A consultant with no industry baggage can often see what insiders can't. Position this as permission to ask "stupid questions" that unlocking value.

2. **Lead with cross-industry pattern recognition.** "I've solved this exact problem in three other industries. Here's what I noticed: the core issue isn't your industry mechanics, it's [underlying pattern]. Once we fix that, your industry-specific tactics get way more effective." Back this up with a specific example from a different vertical.

3. **Show methodology, not just results.** Industry insiders trust process. Show your diagnostic framework, your problem-solving approach, your measurement discipline. If your methodology is sound, it transfers across industries. If it's fluffy, industry experience won't save you anyway. Make the methodology audible in how you describe past work.

4. **Cite specific proof from outside the industry.** "Here's a fractional COO role I did at a healthcare SaaS company. We redesigned their ops from founder-dependent to scalable. This is the same restructure your company needs, and the principles transfer." Be explicit that the example is from a different world. That's the point.

5. **Offer a pilot or lower-stakes entry.** "Let's do a 30-day diagnostic ($5k) before committing to a 6-month engagement. You'll get clarity on whether I'm seeing your core problem correctly, and you'll reduce your risk of bringing in someone new to your industry." Low-risk entry sidesteps the industry objection and lets competence prove itself.

### How to apply it
A fractional Chief Operating Officer (COO) spent 8 years scaling operations at two B2B SaaS companies (10-person to 100-person phases). She now pitches a healthcare device company and hears: "You've never worked in medtech. We have FDA compliance, distribution partners, clinical validation timelines. Your SaaS experience doesn't translate."

She responds: "You're right, I haven't. But here's what I've seen: the core issue is that founders own too many processes, and there's no clarity on who decides what. That slows everything, FDA timelines, partner negotiations, hiring. I solved that at [Company A, SaaS]. I solved it at [Company B, SaaS]. The shape of the problem is identical across medtech, SaaS, and agency services. The FDA piece is your industry tactic. The ownership clarity is the universal issue.

"I'll run a 30-day operational audit. You'll see exactly how I work. If you decide industry experience matters more than this kind of diagnostic rigor, you hire someone from medtech and you know what I'd do differently. Either way, you're not betting your Q2 on someone new to your world."

She gets the 30-day contract. By week 2, the healthcare company's CFO realizes the COO is seeing dysfunction that the healthcare consultants they'd interviewed had normalized as "just how medtech is." The 6-month engagement follows.

### Common traps
- **Being defensive about the objection.** If you push back hard on "you've never worked in our industry," you sound insecure. Accept it. "Yep, I'm new to medtech. Here's why that might be useful. Here's also where I might be wrong. Let's run a pilot to find out." Confidence comes from competence, not from pretending the objection doesn't matter.
- **Citing general cross-industry principles without a specific proof.** "Pattern recognition is valuable in any industry" is true but vague. "I helped three B2B SaaS companies move from chaos to scalable ops, and here's what's analogous to your situation" is specific. Always name the industry and concrete output.
- **Offering to learn on the client's time.** Don't say "I'll ramp up quickly on medtech specifics." Prospects will assume you'll slow them down. Instead: "I'm bringing diagnostic discipline and operational process design. You're bringing medtech expertise. We'll move faster than you could alone." You're bringing value, not arriving with a blank slate.
- **Undervaluing your outsider advantage.** Fresh eyes are a feature, not a limitation. Industry insiders often defend bad practices as "unavoidable" precisely because they've always been that way. An outside consultant can challenge assumptions that insiders won't. Lean into this.
