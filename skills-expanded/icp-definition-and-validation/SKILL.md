# ICP Definition and Validation

### When to use
Before writing a single cold email or sending a LinkedIn connection request. A tight ICP saves months of wasted outreach. If your ICP is "anyone with money," you'll spray-and-pray, burn your reputation, and struggle to refine your message. A specific ICP fixes this.

### The framework
1. **Define core job the buyer must care about.** Not the service you offer, the problem they're trying to solve. Example: "Reduce time spent on manual payroll reconciliation" or "Win more government contracts."
2. **Identify the person who owns that problem.** Job title, seniority, department. A CFO cares about different leverage points than an accounting manager.
3. **Qualify by four fit criteria:** (a) problem fit, do they have the problem at scale?, (b) access fit, can you reach them?, (c) budget fit, do they have money to solve it?, (d) urgency fit, is it bleeding-neck urgent or back-burner?
4. **Screen real prospects to validate, not assumptions.** Find 10 prospects matching your ICP definition. Message 3. Do they engage? Do they confirm the problem exists? Do they fit your access level? Adjust based on response.
5. **Reject vanity ICPs explicitly.** "B2B software companies" is too broad. "Accounting departments at SaaS companies with 50–200 people running Xero where manual month-end reconciliation takes 15+ hours per person" is specific enough to research, message, and refine.

### How to apply it
Let's say you're a fractional CFO targeting consultancies. Your first ICP draft might be "consultancy owners making $50k–$200k per month." Too generic. Let's validate.

Research 10 consultancies in your network. You notice: the ones actually interested in fractional finance support are those who've hit exactly two pain points: (1) they scaled from solo to 3+ people, so accounting became too complex to do on spreadsheets, and (2) they lost a deal or missed a bid because they couldn't quickly pull together cash-flow projections or tax planning. That's your problem fit.

Access fit: you can reach them via LinkedIn (consultancy owners are accessible) and warm intros (most consultants know other consultants). Budget fit: they're turning down $20k–$40k in revenue per year due to admin friction; you charge $2k–$4k/month. Urgency: it's medium-high (they want it solved but aren't in crisis mode).

Now your real ICP: "Service-based consultants (not agencies, not solo founders) doing $100k–$300k annual revenue who've hit the 3–5 person scale inflection point and are using spreadsheets or QuickBooks for accounting." This is specific enough to find them, message them consistently, and measure whether you're winning or failing.

Message three from this bucket. Two respond. One takes a discovery call. That validates problem fit and access fit. You've now earned the right to scale outreach to 50 more like them.

### Common traps
- **Assuming you know the problem better than they do.** You think consultants care about tax optimization; they care about not looking broke in front of a new hire. Ask first, assume second.
- **Confusing "total addressable market" with "people you can convert."** Millions of companies use spreadsheets. Your ICP is the 0.1% of those that (a) know they're inefficient and (b) will pay you to fix it.
- **Mixing job titles and company sizes.** "CEOs at companies with 100+ people" means fortune-500 CEOs act the same as small-business CEOs. They don't. Separate title, size, and industry into independent filters.
- **Validating only with people who already like you.** Your warm network will validate your ICP because they're polite. Find strangers in your ICP and test cold. If cold response rate is <5%, your ICP is off.
- **Changing your ICP after two rejections.** Rejection is normal. Rejection because you're messaging the wrong person is failure. Run 20–30 outreach attempts before deciding the ICP is wrong; run 50 if you're new and may have messaging problems instead.
