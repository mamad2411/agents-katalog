# Roadmapping

## Sequence by value and dependency, not by who asked loudest

A roadmap's job is to say what happens in what order and why — recency or volume of an ask is not a prioritization criterion, however tempting it is under pressure.

## Explicit prioritization criteria

Common axes: business impact, user impact, effort/cost, risk reduction, strategic fit, and hard dependency (can't start until X exists). State which criteria drove a given ranking rather than presenting a final order with no visible reasoning — this is what lets a stakeholder disagree productively instead of just disagreeing with the outcome.

## Honest tradeoffs

A roadmap that fits everything in is a roadmap that hasn't said no to anything — which usually means the sequencing decision was deferred, not made. State explicitly what's being deprioritized and why, not just what's committed.

## Recommendation, not commitment

A roadmap produced through this reasoning is a structured recommendation for the human to confirm — prioritization carries budget, stakeholder, and business-risk accountability that stays with a human decision-maker, not with the analysis that informs it. Present the sequencing, criteria, and tradeoffs completely enough to act on immediately, but don't record anything in project memory as a committed roadmap item until it's actually been confirmed.

## Confidence bands

Near-term roadmap items can be committed with real confidence; far-out items should be flagged as directional, not promised with the same certainty — presenting a 12-month-out item with the same confidence as next-sprint work sets a false expectation.

## Revisiting the roadmap

A roadmap is a living artifact — new information (a validated/falsified hypothesis, a shifted business priority, a discovered risk) should trigger an explicit re-sequencing conversation, not a silent drift where the roadmap stops reflecting reality.
