# /pitch

Use this when you need to explain what you're building clearly and compellingly — or when you need a name for it.

---

## The one-liner scaffold

Don't use this literally. Use it to think.

[Product] helps [specific person] [do the real thing they want] without [the thing they hate].

Be specific about the person. Be honest about what it removes from their life.

---

## Build these in order

**The job to be done.** What is the user actually trying to accomplish? Not the feature. The underlying goal.

**The alternative they use today.** How are they solving this without your product? What's broken about it?

**Your wedge.** What do you do that the alternative doesn't? Be specific. Not "AI-powered." What does the AI actually do?

**The person.** Who specifically is this for? The narrower the better at the start.

**The emotion.** What does it feel like to use your product vs the alternative?

---

## For investors (add these)

Why now? What changed that makes this the right moment?
How does it make money? One sentence.
What have you seen so far? Users, revenue, waitlist, feedback.

---

## Common mistakes

Too broad: "An AI tool for productivity." Means nothing.
Feature-first: leading with how it works instead of what it does for the user.
Jargon: "AI-powered end-to-end portfolio intelligence platform." No.
Underselling: "It's kind of like a portfolio tracker but with some AI stuff." Say the thing.
Over-promising: "The last portfolio app you'll ever need." Nobody believes superlatives.

---

Write the one-liner first. Then the two-paragraph version. Then ask: which part would a potential user find most interesting? Lead with that everywhere.

## Naming the product (or a feature)

Positioning and naming are the same muscle. If you need a memorable handle for a product, feature, or domain, use this process.

### What makes a good name

- **Short.** 1–2 syllables is ideal. 3 is fine. 4+ is hard to remember.
- **Sayable.** Test it: "Hey, have you tried [name]?" If you wouldn't say it confidently, it's not the name.
- **Has a feeling.** The best names carry an emotional tone before you know what the product does.
- **Specific enough to be remembered, vague enough to grow.**
- **Available enough.** Perfect .com availability is a distraction. Check if the .com is taken by an active product in the same space. `[name].app` or `get[name].com` is fine.

### Process

1. **Name the feeling first.** What should someone feel when they say this?
2. **Generate broadly.** Pull from: real words (Notion, Linear, Stripe), made-up words (Figma, Canva), portmanteaus (Instagram, Pinterest), verbs as nouns (Slack, Zoom), foreign words that sound right in English, short phrases made one word (MailChimp).
3. **Filter ruthlessly.** Cut anything hard to spell after hearing it, hard to pronounce after reading it, or already taken by an active product in the same space.
4. **Test the top 3.** Say them out loud. Tell someone without context.

### Quick domain check

For each shortlisted name: search `.com`, `.app`, `.so`. Search the App Store. Google the name alone. Check trademarkia.com for basic conflicts.

### Output

Give 8–12 ideas loosely grouped by feel. Shortlist to top 3 with brief rationale. Then a recommendation.
