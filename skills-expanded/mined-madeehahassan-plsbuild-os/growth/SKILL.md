# /growth

Use this when you're trying to figure out how to get users. Pre-launch, launch day, post-launch, or any time you catch yourself thinking "I built it, why isn't anyone using it."

The problem is almost never the product. The problem is that nobody knows it exists.

## The starting question

Before anything else: **who are you trying to reach, and where do they already spend time?**

Do not start with "how do I market this." Start with "where does my user already hang out, complaining about this exact problem?"

That is your first channel. Go there. Be useful. Do not pitch.

---

## The honest channels audit

For any product, there are maybe 3 channels that actually work. The rest are noise. Before you spread yourself thin, rank them honestly:

- **Search.** Are people Googling this problem? If yes, write the thing they are searching for.
- **Communities.** Is there a subreddit, Discord, Slack, or forum where your users already are? Show up and be helpful before you ever mention your product.
- **Social.** Do your users live on X, LinkedIn, Instagram, TikTok? Pick one. Post where they are, not where you are.
- **Build in public.** Are you building something other builders would want to use, or something that shows what's possible with AI tools? This is your channel. See the section below.
- **Cold outreach.** Can you name 20 specific humans who need this? DM them one by one.
- **Launch platforms.** Product Hunt, Hacker News, Indie Hackers. One-shot bursts, not an ongoing strategy.
- **Word of mouth.** Did your first 5 users tell someone? If not, the product is not good enough yet or they don't know how to describe it.
- **Paid ads.** Almost never right for a solo founder pre-product-market-fit. Skip unless you already have data showing what works.

Pick 2 channels. Commit for 8 weeks. If nothing works in 8 weeks, change the message, not the channel.

## Build in public — if you're a vibe coder or solo builder

If you built something with Claude, Cursor, or any AI tool — and your user is other founders, builders, or people curious about what AI can do — build in public is your highest-leverage channel. Not a side strategy. Your main one.

The audience you're trying to reach is already watching people build things on X, LinkedIn, and Reddit. They want to see the process, the mistakes, the surprising moments. Not polished launches.

**What to post:**

- The problem you noticed and decided to solve (before you built anything)
- A screenshot of the first working version, however rough
- Something that surprised you — a mistake Claude made, a shortcut you found, a decision you changed
- A before/after: what the thing looked like when you started vs. now
- A number: users, signups, time it took to build, something real
- A question: "what would make you actually use this?"

**What not to post:**

- "Excited to announce..." — nobody reads those
- Features nobody asked about
- Vague updates with no visual proof
- Anything that sounds like a press release

**The rhythm that works:**

One post per week. Rotate between: problem post → behind-the-scenes → outcome or learning. Don't go silent for three weeks then blast five posts in a day.

**Where to post:**

X/Twitter first if your audience is builders and indie hackers. LinkedIn if your audience is professionals or founders. Reddit (relevant subreddits) if your audience has a community there. Pick one and be consistent for 8 weeks before adding another.

## Writing posts that find people

The goal of a post is not to sound smart. It is to find the one person who has the problem you solve.

**Structure that works:**

1. Name the problem in the first line, in their words.
2. Show you understand it, specifically.
3. Tell them what you built. One sentence.
4. Show the proof. Screenshot, link, numbers.
5. End with a question or an offer.

Cut everything else.

**What to never do:**

- Do not start with "I am excited to share..."
- Do not write threads that explain your life story before getting to the point.
- Do not announce your product. Announce the problem. The product is the answer.
- Do not pitch. Just show the thing and let people ask.

## Launching

Launch days are low-information. Most launches flop for reasons that have nothing to do with the product.

**Before you launch:**

1. Get 20 people who actually care to commit to showing up on launch day.
2. Write a post that is not an announcement. Write about the problem.
3. Have one clear thing for visitors to do when they arrive. Not three.
4. Turn off everything else you were planning to ship that week.

**On launch day:**

1. Post in the morning, in your audience's timezone.
2. Reply to every comment within an hour, all day.
3. DM every single person who engaged and ask what made them click.
4. Do not check the counter. It does not matter.

**After the launch:**

Read every comment. The angry ones matter more than the happy ones. The silent users matter most.

## Traps to avoid

**The shotgun approach.** Being on every platform at once. Pick two. Be great on those.

**Hiding behind building.** If you keep polishing instead of telling anyone, the problem is fear of rejection, not the product. Ship anyway.

**Vanity metrics.** Likes, followers, impressions. None of those pay rent. Track: emails collected, messages received, paying users, referrals.

**Waiting for perfect.** Nothing is ever ready. Ship at "slightly embarrassing." If you wait longer, someone else ships a worse version and gets the market.

**Posting without engaging.** Dropping a post and disappearing is half the work. Comment on other people's posts in your space for two weeks before you post anything promotional. Show up as a person first.
