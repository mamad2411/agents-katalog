# Brevity Briefing

Headline, one new fact, why it matters to this reader, then stop.

Source: the book *Smart Brevity* and Axios's public guides to it. This skill is not an Axios product and has no connection to Axios.

## The core rule

**Tell one chosen reader what is new and why it matters to them, first, in labeled lines they can scan.** Readers decide fast whether a message is about something relevant to them. This format answers that question in the first lines, then cuts everything the reader already knows.

Workflow: `pick one real reader` → `write the one-sentence "what's new"` → `write the headline` → `write "Why it matters:" for that reader` → `add labeled bullets only if needed` → `cut` → `run the Verify checklist`.

## Mechanics

1. **Reader first.** Before you write, name the goal, the ideal reader, and one real person who fits. Keep only what is new, interesting, or urgent to that person. Cut what they already know.
2. **Headline: 10 words or 60 characters, maximum.** Make it declarative, with at least one concrete fact the reader learns from the headline alone. Use conversational words. No clickbait. "Our remote work plans through year-end", not "Update on our plans".
3. **What's new: the first sentence, one sentence only.** It holds the single detail you want remembered and stands alone as the first paragraph. Find it with the barstool test: what would you say first to your boss as the elevator door closes? "Acme just signed a $2 million contract!"
4. **Headline and what's new build, never repeat.** The first sentence adds a new fact to the headline. If they say the same thing, rewrite one.
5. **Write "Why it matters:" on the page.** Put the literal label, in bold, at the start of the second paragraph. Follow it with one or two tight sentences on the impact for the reader you chose, not for you. The sentence changes with the reader: an executive gets the revenue effect, a peer gets the workload effect.
6. **Label the rest with axioms.** Open each further block with a short bold label that tells the scanner what kind of point follows. Labels Axios uses in its own pages: **The big picture:**, **Yes, but:**, **Reality check:**, **The bottom line:**, **Go deeper**. Use a label only when the content fits it.
7. **Go deeper goes last.** If a longer source exists, end with **Go deeper:** and a pointer to it, as Axios's own guides do. The reader then chooses whether to read more. If no source exists, leave it out.
8. **Style for scanning.** Short paragraphs, bullets for parallel details, white space between blocks. Bold the labels and the few words a scanner must catch; nothing else.
9. **Write like you talk.** Subject, verb, object. Short words. No word you would not say to the reader over coffee. "Lying", not "prevaricating".
10. **Stop when enough is enough.** Use as few words, sentences, and paragraphs as possible. Axios's data says you will be lucky to keep a reader for 200 words, so treat 200 words as the ceiling for the body. Short, not shallow: keep any context the reader needs to act or understand.

## Verify

- The headline has 10 words or fewer and 60 characters or fewer, and states a concrete fact
- The first sentence is the what's new, is one sentence, and is its own paragraph
- The first sentence adds a fact the headline does not already state
- The literal bold label **Why it matters:** opens the second paragraph and has one or two sentences
- The why-it-matters text names an effect on the chosen reader, not on the writer
- Every later block opens with a bold label that fits its content
- **Go deeper** appears only if a real link or document exists, and only at the end
- The body has 200 words or fewer, and parallel details sit in bullets, not in one paragraph
- No fact, number, or name appears that the source material does not contain
- Last step: run the `de-ai` skill on the draft before you deliver it

## Do not

- Use the book title or the Axios name as a label or product name for your output
- Open with background, history, or "I wanted to share a quick update"
- Write why it matters to you ("we worked hard on this") instead of to the reader
- Stack labels on thin content; a label with nothing under it is noise
- Cut context the reader needs just to hit the word ceiling
- Bold whole sentences or every other phrase; bold loses its signal

## Example transformations

**Before:** "Hi all, I wanted to share a quick update on the office move. As many of you know, we have been looking at options for a while now, and after a lot of discussion with facilities and leadership, we have decided to move to the new building on Main Street. The move will happen on March 14. Everyone will need to pack their desks by March 12. IT will set up computers over the weekend so they are ready on Monday."

**After:**

**Office moves to Main Street on March 14**

You need to pack your desk by March 12.

**Why it matters:** IT sets up computers over the weekend, so they are ready on Monday.

- Leadership and facilities chose the new building after a long look at options.

**Before:** "The Q3 customer survey results came in last week. Overall satisfaction rose from 72% to 81%. However, response rates fell from 30% to 18%, so the sample is smaller than last quarter. The biggest complaint was slow support response times, mentioned by 40% of respondents. The full report is in the shared drive under Surveys/Q3."

**After:**

**Customers are happier, but support is too slow**

Satisfaction in the Q3 customer survey rose from 72% to 81%.

**Why it matters:** Slow support response times were the biggest complaint, named by 40% of respondents.

**Yes, but:** Response rates fell from 30% to 18%, so the sample is smaller than last quarter.

**Go deeper:** The full report is in the shared drive under Surveys/Q3.
