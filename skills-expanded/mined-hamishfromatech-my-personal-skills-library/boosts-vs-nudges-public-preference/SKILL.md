# Boosts vs. Nudges: Public Preference and Perceived Effectiveness

## Overview

The nudge-vs-boost debate has been framed as a theoretical question — which intervention works better? Paunov & Grüne-Yanoff (2026, Energy Policy) demonstrate it is also a public perception question: people's preference between boosts and nudges is shaped by how effective they perceive each to be and how much criticism each has received. This has direct implications for A-Tech's behavioral design strategy: the boosting approach is theoretically superior for building lasting competence, but its adoption depends on communicating effectiveness and managing the perception that boosts require more time/effort.

## When to Use

- Designing behavior change interventions where public/user acceptance matters
- Communicating the choice between nudging and boosting to stakeholders
- Defending a boosting-first product design against "just nudge them" pressure
- Setting behavioral policy that accounts for perceived effectiveness, not just theoretical effectiveness
- Designing communication that increases boost acceptance (addressing time/effort perception)

## Core Process / Workflow

### Step 1: Understand the Finding

Paunov & Grüne-Yanoff (2026) studied what shapes public preference between boosts and nudges for sustainable behavioral policies. Key findings:

| Factor | Effect on Preference | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Perceived effectiveness** | Higher perceived effectiveness increases preference for that intervention | The theoretically superior intervention wins only if perceived as effective |
| **Perceived time/effort cost** | Higher perceived time/effort costs REDUCE preference for boosts (B = −0.568, p = .007, OR = 0.57) | Boosts are disadvantaged by the perception that they require more user effort |
| **Criticism of intervention** | Criticism reduces preference for the criticized type | Nudges lose preference when criticized (the reckoning); boosts lose when perceived as effortful |

**The core insight:** The nudge-boost choice is not just a theoretical question — it's a perception and communication problem. Even if boosts are theoretically superior, they lose to nudges if perceived as too effortful or if their effectiveness isn't communicated.

### Step 2: Diagnose the Boost Disadvantage

Boosts face a structural perception problem:

| Dimension | Nudge | Boost |
|---|---|---|
| **Perceived effort** | Low (just change the default) | High (teach me a skill) |
| **Perceived speed** | Fast (immediate behavior change) | Slow (competence takes time) |
| **Perceived convenience** | Done to you | Requires your participation |
| **Actual durability** | Often non-persistent | Durable and transferable |
| **Theoretical superiority** | For one-time, low-attention behaviors | For high-stakes, deliberate behaviors |

**The trap:** Boosts are the better long-term intervention, but they lose the perception battle on effort and speed — the very dimensions where nudges appear to win.

### Step 3: Design Communication That Increases Boost Acceptance

To overcome the perception disadvantage, communication must reframe three dimensions:

**1. Reframe effort as investment:**
- Don't say: "Boosts require more time and effort"
- Say: "Boosts build a skill that pays dividends for life; nudges change one decision"
- Anchor on the durability and transferability advantage

**2. Reframe speed as sustainability:**
- Don't say: "Nudges work faster"
- Say: "Nudges work once; boosts work forever"
- The time-to-effect is longer, but the effect duration is dramatically longer

**3. Reframe participation as empowerment:**
- Don't say: "Boosts require your active involvement"
- Say: "Boosts give you control; nudges decide for you"
- Align with A-Tech's autonomy and agency values

### Step 4: Choose the Right Intervention Based on Context

The finding implies a context-dependent strategy:

| Context | Preferred Intervention | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **One-time, low-attention behavior** | Nudge | Speed matters; competence not needed |
| **High-stakes, deliberate behavior** | Boost | Durability matters; competence required |
| **Public acceptance critical** | Hybrid: nudge to start, boost to sustain | Nudge overcomes the perception barrier; boost builds lasting competence |
| **Time-constrained users** | Nudge first | Boost perception disadvantage is real |
| **Autonomy-valuing users** | Boost | Aligns with user agency values |

### Step 5: Apply to A-Tech's Boosting-First Strategy

A-Tech's boosting-first approach (captured in boosting-empowering-behavior-change) is theoretically superior but faces the perception disadvantage this study identifies. The response:

**Product communication:**
- Frame Be Practical modules as skill investments, not effort costs
- Show the durability advantage: "Learn this once, use it for life"
- Contrast with nudge-based products: "Other tools decide for you; we teach you to decide"

**Behavioral design:**
- Use nudges to clear the path (reduce friction) AND boosts to build the traveler (build competence)
- The hybrid approach overcomes the perception disadvantage: the nudge gets users started; the boost builds lasting capability

**Policy defense:**
- When challenged with "just nudge them," cite the durability and transferability evidence
- When challenged with "boosts take too long," cite the non-persistence of nudge effects
- Frame the choice as investment vs. convenience, not effort vs. ease

## The Three-Stage Hybrid Strategy

Based on the perception findings, the optimal A-Tech approach is a three-stage hybrid:

| Stage | Intervention | Goal | Perception Challenge Addressed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1: Onboard** | Nudge (defaults, social proof, friction reduction) | Get started fast | Overcomes "boosts are too slow" |
| **Stage 2: Build** | Boost (skill-building, competence development) | Lasting capability | Overcomes "nudges don't persist" |
| **Stage 3: Sustain** | Self-nudging (user becomes their own choice architect) | User autonomy | Combines both: fast + durable |

## A-Tech Application

### A-Coder
- Use nudges for onboarding (sensible defaults, progressive disclosure) to overcome the "too much effort" perception
- Use boosts for skill development (teach developers to evaluate AI code, not just accept it)
- The paradox of supervision (needing skills AI erodes) is a boost argument — build the skill, don't just nudge the behavior

### Be Practical
- Frame financial literacy modules as investments ("learn once, use for life"), not effort costs
- Use the three-stage hybrid: nudge to start (auto-enroll in modules), boost to build (interactive simulations), self-nudge to sustain (user sets their own defaults)
