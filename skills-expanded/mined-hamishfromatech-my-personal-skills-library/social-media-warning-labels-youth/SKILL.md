# Social-Media Warning Labels: What Wording Works for Young People

## The policy moment meets the evidence gap

Four US states (California, Colorado, Minnesota, New York) have passed social-media warning laws — **California's takes effect Jan 1, 2027** — with bills pending in Texas, Ohio and Pennsylvania, following the 2024 Surgeon General call and a 42-state-AG bipartisan petition. But until this study, nobody had rigorously tested what warnings should say or whether they're promising. Grummon, Robinson and colleagues (Stanford Medicine, with UNC and UC Davis; funded by the Stanford Maternal and Child Health Research Institute) tested **14 warning messages plus a control** on 1,012 young people (half teens 13–17, half young adults 18–29), formatted as Instagram pop-ups, measuring two outcomes: "How much does this message discourage you from wanting to use social media?" and "How much does this message increase your awareness of the harms?"

## Findings

1. **Every warning beat control.** All 14 messages (topics: addiction, body image, sleep, depression, anxiety, overall mental-health harms, "not proven safe"; two wordings each) rated as more effective than a seatbelt-safety control message.
2. **Mental-health framing resonates most.** The most effective were **overall mental-health harms, depression, and anxiety** — harms young people can see in their own lives ("That's right, I can see that happening to my friends or to me," as Grummon put it).
3. **Health claims beat generic friction.** All health-effect warnings *except the addiction message* were rated more effective than a screen-time-break prompt of the kind platforms already deploy — evidence that platform-native "take a break" nudges are the weaker instrument.
4. **Teens and young adults responded alike** — notable given how fast the digital landscape changes across even a few years of age.
5. **California's statutory wording held its own** despite being longer and more legally burdened ("…while social media may have benefits for some young users, social media is associated with significant mental health harms and has not been proven safe for young users"). The drafters' priority was surviving industry litigation; the data suggests the compromise cost little.

## Design rules for digital-wellbeing warnings

1. **Name the mental-health harm specifically** — depression/anxiety wording outranked generic "harms" and addiction framing in perceived effectiveness.
2. **Attribute to a credible source** — Surgeon General's Warning framing carried the evidence weight; the wording discipline (topic pairs) matters ("Sometimes, subtle changes to warnings really matter").
3. **Health content > friction nudges** — if the choice is a warning vs a "take a break" prompt, the evidence favors the warning for shifting intent.
4. **Expect the pop-up format to change the mechanism** — unlike tobacco labels that live on the product, these warnings are intermittent pop-ups; the behavior-change pathway (and its decay) is genuinely unknown.
5. **Litigation-proofing and effectiveness are compatible** — California's compromise wording performed on par; legal robustness need not cost persuasiveness.

## Honest caveats

- **Perceived effectiveness ≠ behavior change.** Participants rated what messages *would* do; the authors' explicit next step is testing whether warnings actually reduce use. Warning-label effects for tobacco/alcohol are established, but social-media pop-ups are a different mechanism.
- Self-report, online panel, single exposure ratings; no longitudinal exposure data.
- All messages were tested on users of an Instagram-formatted page — platform-specific presentation effects unmeasured.
- The addiction warning's relative weakness may reflect message fatigue or perceived irrelevance rather than topic inferiority.
- State laws are under industry litigation; implementation details (which warnings, where shown) remain unsettled.
