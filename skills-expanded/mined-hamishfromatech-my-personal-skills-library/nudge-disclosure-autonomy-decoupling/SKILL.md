# Disclosure Decoupling: Transparency Doesn't Buy Autonomy Back

**Source:** Cuypers, Raymaekers & Van de Walle (KU Leuven), "Unpacking transparency in nudging: the impact of different disclosure messages on nudge effectiveness and perceived autonomy," Behavioural Public Policy, published May 28 2026 (open access). Preregistered vignette experiment, n=1,916, six arms: control, nudge-only, and four disclosure types (presence / purpose / mechanism / combined) on a sustainable-food salience nudge. TOST equivalence testing on all nulls.

## Core Findings (all three are decision-relevant)

1. **The nudge worked:** +10.4 pp sustainable choices vs control.
2. **Every disclosure type left effectiveness unchanged** — none enhanced, none reduced it (statistically equivalent to nudge-only). No reactance.
3. **The nudge itself reduced perceived autonomy (−2.4 pp), and no disclosure offset that loss.** Even the "combined" disclosure (presence + purpose + mechanism, the fullest transparency package) failed to restore autonomy.
4. **Attention to disclosures was poor:** ~40% failed the awareness checks (both nudge awareness and disclosure awareness). If disclosures go unnoticed, they can't increase transparency.

## Design Rule (the transferable principle)

**Decouple the two questions:** "Does transparency hurt effectiveness?" (no) and "Does transparency restore autonomy?" (no). Practitioners have historically conflated them — assuming that disclosing a nudge is the ethical fix because it both preserves effect and repairs autonomy. The evidence says disclosure is *effectiveness-neutral* but *autonomy-inert*. Autonomy damage from being nudged appears to come from the manipulation itself, not from ignorance of it.

**Implications:**
- Disclose when transparency is required (ethics, regulation, trust-building) — you won't lose the effect.
- Don't disclose *expecting* an autonomy or trust dividend — the evidence does not support that payoff.
- For interventions where autonomy matters substantively (vulnerable users, high-stakes domains, AI companions), rely on boosts/choice-architecture changes that actually increase capability, not on disclosure labels.
- **For AI products:** a "we may nudge you" banner is compliance theater unless the intervention itself is autonomy-preserving (user-configurable, opt-out, boost-style). This connects directly to consent design.

## A-Tech Fit

- **Practical implementation:** adds a cheap, citable evidence point to any A-Tech trust/consent recommendation: disclosure is a compliance tool, not an autonomy tool.
- **Privacy:** reframes consent UX — telling users about personalization ≠ restoring their sense of control; control must be functional (opt-out, user-visible, revocable).
- **Open science:** preregistered, OSF data available; replicable vignette pattern for A/B-testing disclosure copy.
- **Financial freedom:** prevents wasted spend on "transparency theater" that doesn't move the autonomy metric that actually matters.
