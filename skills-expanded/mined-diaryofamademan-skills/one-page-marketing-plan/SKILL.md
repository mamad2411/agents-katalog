# <img src="assets/icons/check-list.svg" width="20" height="20" alt="" /> 1-Page Marketing Plan

> **How to read this**
>
> Most marketing plans fail in one of two directions. The tactic pile: nine boxes filled with activities, no diagnosis underneath, nothing connecting them — motion that cannot say why it should work. Or the evidence pile: correct findings about buyers that never become a decision anyone can act on Monday morning. This skill takes the disciplined middle. The shape is Allan Dib's nine squares — three phases, Before, During, After, one page, a sentence or two per square. The order of work is Mark Ritson's: diagnosis before strategy before tactics. And each square stands on measured evidence about how buyers behave — the Ehrenberg-Bass buying laws, the IPA effectiveness databank, lead-response audits, lifetime-value models, referral-program data — with every rule labeled for where it was measured, because the user's own numbers are the court of appeal.

## What this skill is

A marketing plan is a set of choices about a system: who the business is trying to reach, what it will say, where it will say it, how a stranger becomes a lead and a lead becomes a customer, and what a customer is worth afterward. This skill builds that system on one page, in a fixed order, and refuses two common cheats. It refuses to fill the grid before the diagnosis — a plan written without one is tactics in nine boxes. And it refuses to let any square stand on a guru's assertion when the square has been measured: follow-up speed has audit data behind it, lifetime value has probability models behind it, referrals have a tracked bank cohort behind it. Where the evidence is practice rather than measurement, the plan says so.

The grid also carries one layer across all nine squares: Les Binet and Peter Field's finding that brand building and sales activation are different jobs running on different clocks. The Before squares mostly do the brand job — building memory among people who are not buying today. The During squares do the activation job — harvesting the demand that exists now. The finished plan states the split between the two as an explicit line, held as a hypothesis, because a plan that only harvests will find each year's harvest thinner than the last.

## When to use

- **A business needs a marketing plan it can hold on one page** — a founder, a small team, a solo operator — where the whole system must be visible at once and every element must earn its place.

- **An existing plan is a tactic pile** — channels and campaigns with no diagnosis, no stated split, and no standards — and needs rebuilding from the buyer evidence up.

- **A positioning record exists and needs carrying to market** — the concept and the frame are decided; what is missing is the plan that puts them in front of buyers and turns response into revenue.

- **A budget decision is being made** — how much effort builds future demand versus harvests present demand — and the decision needs to be stated, dated, and made reviewable instead of drifted into.

## When not to use

- **Not before positioning, if positioning is the real gap.** If the business cannot yet say what it is, for whom, and instead of what, that is the positioning skill's work. This skill's message square consumes a positioning record; it does not rebuild one (see Square 2).

- **Not to write the words.** How a public moment is said — the post, the page, the announcement — is the comms skill's ground. This skill plans the system the moments live in: which moments exist, in what order, aimed at whom, measured how.

- **Not as a substitute for the numbers.** The plan sets starting settings from imported evidence. A business with its own cohort data, response-time audits, and asset tests should already be running on those; this skill's later steps say how the plan hands over to them.

- **Not for a single campaign.** One launch, one offer, one season is a tactic. This skill builds the standing system tactics plug into.

## The procedure

### Step 0 — Diagnose first

No square is filled until the diagnosis exists. The full method is in `references/diagnosis-first.md`; the shape is Richard Rumelt's kernel, run in Mark Ritson's order.

- **Diagnosis.** What is actually happening: the category and how it is bought (how often, by how many, how lightly), the business's own numbers where they exist, and the specific obstacle the plan must overcome. A diagnosis names a difficulty; it is not a list of goals.

- **Guiding policy.** The choice that answers the diagnosis — an approach with a sacrifice inside it. Choosing a first segment is a guiding policy. So is choosing memory over argument, or speed of follow-up over a new channel. A policy that costs nothing chooses nothing.

- **Coherent action.** The nine squares, filled so each one serves the policy and reinforces the others. Coherence is the test: a square that contradicts the policy is rewritten or the policy is.

Ritson's Bothism referees the grid's standing arguments — niche versus penetration, brand versus activation, difference versus recognition. The skill's rule: these are sequencing and weighting questions, not camps. Where the roster's sources pull against each other, the plan states both, picks a starting position, and names the user's own data as the judge.

### Step 1 — Before: prospects (squares 1–3)

The brand job. Most of this phase's audience is not buying today — John Dawes's 95-5 rule: in any period, only a small share of a category's buyers are in market, because buying cycles are long. This phase builds the memory those buyers will act on later, and it is judged on that clock, not this quarter's.

**Square 1 — Select target market.** Name a first segment and the wider category it sits in. The segment is where the plan starts — a small budget spread across a whole category reaches nobody often enough to register — and it is never where the plan ends: growth comes from penetration, from more category buyers, most of them light buyers, so a niche ceiling is a growth ceiling. The square states the broadening trigger: the condition (share of the first segment, a budget level, a date) on which the target widens. If a positioning record exists, its Best-fit customers seed the first segment. Evidence: the Ehrenberg-Bass buying laws — penetration and light buyers — fitted to decades of consumer panel data across categories and countries.

**Square 2 — Craft your message.** Two jobs, in order. First, be recognized: the message is carried by distinctive assets — colors, sounds, characters, phrases, formats — tested on Jenni Romaniuk's two measures, Fame (how many category buyers link the asset to any brand) and Uniqueness (how many link it to this brand only), and kept consistent rather than redesigned away; buyers perceive very little difference between brands, and memory rewards recognition, not argument. Second, say one clear thing to the chosen segment, with honest proof inside it — real reviews, real counts, real credentials (Robert Cialdini's principles as design material, at their real, single-digit effect sizes). If the message must travel, Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman's sharing data decides its contents: practical value and high-arousal emotion travel; sadness and self-congratulation do not.

Square 2 declares its input. When a positioning record exists, this square consumes it and does not re-derive it. The record's fields, and what the square takes from each:

| Field | What square 2 takes from it |
| --- | --- |
| Owned concept | The message itself — the one idea every execution must land and be remembered for |
| Market category | The frame the message speaks inside — the words buyers themselves use for the choice |
| Competitive frame | The "unlike what" contrast — the alternatives named, the status quo included |
| Best-fit customers | Whose square this is — who square 1 starts from, and who the message is phrased for first |
| Trade-offs | The bound on the message — what it may never promise, the sacrifices the position refuses to give up |

When no positioning record exists, square 2 runs a light message step — one clear thing for the named segment, the asset inventory taken, proof listed — and names the positioning skill as the deeper route. The plan is marked as carrying a light square 2 until a record replaces it.

**Square 3 — Reach prospects with media.** Buy attention-adjusted reach against all category buyers. Byron Sharp's discipline says reach broadly — mental availability (being thought of in buying situations) plus physical availability (being easy to buy). Karen Nelson-Field's measurements correct the counting: about 85 percent of digital ads receive under 2.5 seconds of active attention, and memory encoding starts at roughly 1.5 to 2.5 seconds of real attention — so reach is planned in environments and formats where the brand's assets actually get their seconds, with the assets arriving in the first seconds of every execution. Dawes's rule sets the expectation: most of the people reached are out of market today, and the reach is for their future buying moment. The square states the user's own in-market ratio, computed from their category's buying cycle, rather than importing 95-5 as a constant. Each channel and surface in the square also names the awareness stage it speaks to — unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, or most aware — taken from the positioning record's customer awareness stage when a record exists, and from the Step 0 diagnosis when none does: a surface aimed at the wrong stage spends its seconds on buyers who cannot yet hear it. Each channel and surface likewise states its realistic attention expectation — the active seconds it can honestly expect, set from measured attention evidence rather than platform promises — and the square's reach is planned in those seconds: attention is treated as measured, never assumed, the first moments of every execution are where the expectation is won or lost, and a buy counted in impressions nobody looked at fails the square however large the number. Caveat, carried in the plan: Nelson-Field founded the company that sells attention measurement; her thresholds are direction, and the user's own results arbitrate. And media is bought in auctions against competitors who react — bid to the value of the attention, not to win the auction; formats copy in a week, so the distinctive assets, not the format, are the defensible part of the buy.

### Step 2 — During: leads (squares 4–6)

The activation job. Demand exists now, in the small in-market share, and this phase's losses are mostly operational: slow replies, thin follow-up, pressure where memory-keeping was needed.

**Square 4 — Capture leads.** The capture mechanism, the consent it stands on, and a response-time standard written as a number. James Oldroyd's research is the load-bearing evidence: in an audit that sent a test lead to 2,241 US companies, only 37 percent replied within an hour, the average reply took 42 hours, and about a quarter never replied at all; across 1.25 million real leads, firms that tried contact within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that waited one more hour, and over sixty times as likely as firms that waited a day. The standard the plan sets: a first contact attempt inside the hour, inside business hours, with the user's own response times audited against it. Consent is carried by law and by deliverability practice — permission before contact, messages the recipient has reason to expect — as operating rules, not as anyone's slogan. One folklore flag, stated wherever this research is cited: the famous "391 percent" figure attached to lead response comes from a software vendor's separate analysis, not from Oldroyd's study, and it never appears in a plan this skill produces.

**Square 5 — Nurture leads.** Memory-keeping, not pressure. Most leads are out of market when they arrive — that is Dawes's rule applied at the level of the list — so the nurture cadence exists to keep the brand's assets familiar until the buying moment arrives, at a rhythm the recipient can stand, with something of practical value in it often enough to earn the next opening. The failure mode is the pressure sequence: escalating urgency aimed at people who were never going to buy this month, which spends the familiarity the cadence was supposed to build. The square states the cadence, the value it carries, and the exit — how a lead leaves the sequence, and what a buying signal looks like when one finally appears.

**Square 6 — Convert sales.** Follow-up first: the conversion leak is usually not the offer but slow, thin follow-up, so the square restates the response standard and the attempt cadence from squares 4 and 5 before it touches the offer. Then conversion design from Cialdini's principles, honest versions only — real social proof (actual customers, actual counts), real scarcity (actual limits), real deadlines (actual dates), authority that was earned — with expectations set at what the studies support: single-digit percentage effects, not levers that make people say yes. Proof races are a named risk: when every competitor claims five stars, unverifiable proof inflates to worthlessness, so the plan prefers proof a buyer can check. Manufactured proof, invented counts, and fake deadlines are out, as a guardrail, not a preference.

### Step 3 — After: customers (squares 7–9)

Margin, retention economics, and referral yield — measured, and fenced off from the growth job. The penetration evidence caps what this phase may promise: loyalty follows brand size and cannot be bought on its own, and the top fifth of buyers supply about half of sales, not the folklore four-fifths. Growth targets stay in the Before squares; this phase is judged on what a customer is worth and what a customer brings.

**Square 7 — Deliver a world-class experience.** Define the experience in observable terms — what the customer can check, not adjectives the business awards itself: the thing delivered, the time it takes, what happens when something goes wrong. This square stands on Dib's practice discipline and is bounded by the penetration cap above; it also carries one measured condition from the referral research: a referred customer's loyalty partly depends on the referrer still being a customer, so the experience of existing customers is part of the referral program's machinery, not a separate virtue. The square names the two or three experience facts the business will be able to prove, because those facts are what squares 8 and 9 spend.

**Square 8 — Increase lifetime value.** Estimate it; never assert it. Peter Fader's models (with Bruce Hardie) estimate a customer's future value from recency and frequency — who bought lately, who buys often, who has quietly left — fitted to real transaction data and checked against holdout periods the models never saw. The square states the measurement before any spending: the cohort view of the current base, the estimated value distribution, and which customers are actually worth more. Fader and the Ehrenberg-Bass models disagree here — Fader holds that top customers are worth more than a static model of buying implies, because customers change over time — and the plan does not referee that argument from authority: it measures the user's own base and lets the holdout test arbitrate. Any lifetime-value figure in the plan carries its method next to it.

**Square 9 — Orchestrate referrals.** A program with measured economics, not a hope and not a slogan. Philipp Schmitt, Bernd Skiera, and Christophe Van den Bulte tracked some 10,000 customers of a German bank for nearly three years: customers won through the referral program were worth at least 16 percent more than comparable customers won other ways, and churned about 18 percent less — a gap that held — while the margin gap faded. The bank paid a 25 euro reward for a customer worth about 40 euros more, and that is how a reward ceiling is set: the square states the measured value gap between referred and other customers, and caps the reward below it. The program aims selectively — at customers whose own experience (square 7) gives them something true to pass on — and Berger's sharing evidence designs the moment: people pass on practical value and things that make them look good to their peers, so the ask is built around both. Referral rewards are visible and copyable, so the durable edge is the measured gap and the referrer relationship, never the reward's size.

### Step 4 — State the split, and set the checks

The layer over the grid, made explicit. The full treatment is in `references/the-split.md`.

- **The split line.** The finished plan states what share of effort and budget builds memory among people not buying yet (the brand job) and what share harvests the demand that exists (the activation job). Starting hypotheses from Binet and Field's databank: roughly 60:40 brand-to-activation in general, nearer 46:54 in business-to-business. The caveat travels with the numbers: the databank is IPA award entries — 996 cases, 700 brands, from 1980 on — not a random sample of campaigns, and the plan treats the ratio as a question its own results will answer, with a review date written next to it.

- **The fence.** After-phase spending does not silently eat the acquisition budget. Any change to the split is a stated decision with a date, not a drift.

- **The checks.** Each imported rule carries the date or condition on which the user's own numbers replace it: the response-time audit against the square 4 standard, the asset test on Fame and Uniqueness, the cohort measurement behind square 8, the referral value gap behind square 9. A plan whose checks never run is a hypothesis that refused its own test.

## Guardrails

1. **Diagnosis before squares.** No grid is filled before Step 0 produces a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and the coherence test. A plan that starts at square 1 is tactics in nine boxes.

2. **Square 2 does not rebuild positioning.** A positioning record, when one exists, is consumed as given — all five fields. When none exists, the light message step runs and the plan names the positioning skill as the deeper route. The plan never quietly upgrades a light square 2 into a settled position.

3. **No folklore numbers.** The "391 percent" lead-response figure (a vendor's analysis, not the study), the 80/20 customer-value split (the measured figure is nearer half of sales from the top fifth), the claim that keeping 5 percent more customers nearly doubles profits (no source data was ever produced for it), and any score presented as a growth law without its evidence — none of these appear in a plan, even as decoration.

4. **Honest proof only.** Social proof, scarcity, and deadlines in any square are real or they are absent. Manufactured counts, invented reviews, and fake urgency are out — they are also, on the evidence, a wasting asset: buyers discount proof they cannot check.

5. **Every imported rule carries its geography.** Where the evidence was measured — packaged-goods panels, UK award cases, a German bank, US inbound leads, business buying cycles — is stated next to the rule, and the user's own numbers are the court of appeal, with the handover checks named in Step 4.

6. **The split is a hypothesis, never a constant.** It is stated, dated, and reviewed against the plan's own results. Presenting 60:40 or 46:54 as a law of marketing is a misread of a databank of award entries.

7. **One page.** The plan fits on one page, a sentence or two per square, readable at a glance. If it does not fit, the choices have not been made yet — length is where unmade choices hide.

8. **After money stays fenced.** Spending in the After squares is justified by measured customer value (square 8) and measured referral yield (square 9), and it does not draw on the Before budget without a stated change to the split.

## Calls on sibling skills

This skill does not run alone; the playbook is one playbook, and a skill calls the siblings its work needs, by name, at the step that needs them. The calls are steps, not suggestions:

- **At Squares 1–2, call positioning** — the target market and the message rest on the adopted position; if none exists, positioning runs first and the plan waits.

- **At Square 3's creative, call stop-the-scroll** — channel copy must earn attention in the active seconds the square records, under that skill's tests.

- **At the plan's economics, call business-model-canvas** — when no current canvas exists, the model the plan serves is drawn first; a plan for an unstated model is a budget in search of a business.

## Output contract

Every run returns one page, plus the notes that keep it honest:

1. **The diagnosis and the guiding policy.** Three or four lines: what is happening, the obstacle, the choice and its sacrifice. No squares without this above them.

2. **The nine squares, in Dib's order.** Before (1–3), During (4–6), After (7–9), one or two sentences each, in the order a buyer meets them. Square 2 shows its source: the positioning record it consumed, by the five field names — or the light-step marker and the pointer to the positioning skill.

3. **The split line.** Brand share, activation share, the starting hypothesis they came from, the databank caveat in one clause, and the review date.

4. **The standards and measures.** The response-time standard (square 4), the user's own in-market ratio and how it was computed (square 3), the asset test status (square 2), the lifetime-value method (square 8), the referral reward cap and the measured gap it sits under (square 9).

5. **The evidence notes.** For each imported rule the plan leans on: where it was measured, and the check on which the user's own numbers replace it.

**The glance test.** The page is read the way a buyer reads anything: in seconds, once. A reader who gives it one pass comes away with the diagnosis, the nine moves, and the split. If a square needs a second reading, it is rewritten, not explained.

**Then run the writing pass. This step calls two sibling skills. It is a procedure, not a style reminder.** When a marketing plan ships in raw strategy register, the nine squares read as a consultant's grid and the diagnosis and split — the decisions the reader has to judge — hide behind imported terms.

1. **Humanizer.** Run the work-humanizer skill in rewrite mode over the 1-page plan and its evidence notes. Facts, figures, quotes, names, dates, and statuses never change in the pass. What changes is the register.

2. **Plain style.** Apply the writing-styles skill's plain rules to the result: short sentences, one idea each, numbers spoken the way a person says them, bold only on the few facts that matter.

3. **The gate.** The plan is not done until the pass has run on the exact text being delivered.

Numbers, ratios, and source findings stay exact — evidence never gets paraphrased into softness. Write bulleted lists loose, a blank line between items, so each point stands on its own. Where a plan will be shown to investors, the hypothesis labels travel with it: the split and the ratios are stated as hypotheses in the plan, and they are never restated as forecasts anywhere the plan is cited.

## Limits and boundaries

- **The grid itself is practice, not measurement.** Dib's nine squares are a practitioner's framework, grade (b): the sequence and the 1-page discipline are the contribution, and the grid as a whole has never been independently tested. Some of its sub-claims — a scored index for picking a market, niche-always — are the author's assertion, and this skill does not carry them. Each square stands on the evidence named in it, not on the grid's authority.

- **Evidence geography.** Almost every measured source was measured somewhere specific: the buying laws on packaged-goods and consumer panels, the split on UK award cases, follow-up speed on US inbound leads, referral value on one German bank's customers, the market-timing rule on business buying cycles. A given user — a small firm, a subscription product, a local service — sits at the edge of several of these datasets at once and squarely inside none. The skill's answer is labeling and handover (Step 4), not false confidence.

- **A vendor caveat stands in the media square.** The attention thresholds come from a research program whose founder sells attention measurement. The direction — attention, not impressions, is the scarce thing — is corroborated by the memory findings; the exact thresholds are used as direction, and the user's own results arbitrate.

- **Boundary with the positioning skill.** Positioning owns the concept and the frame: its record's five fields — Owned concept, Market category, Competitive frame, Best-fit customers, Trade-offs. This skill owns the plan that carries them to market. Square 2 is the seam: it consumes the record, and where the record is thin, the plan says so and points back — it does not fill the gap itself.

- **Boundary with the comms skill.** Comms owns public moments and how a thing is said. This skill plans the system the moments live in — which moments exist, in what order, aimed at whom, carrying which assets, measured how. A plan produced here will name moments; the words of the moments are comms' work, and the plan's message square constrains them without writing them.

- **Boundary with the fundraising skill.** Where a plan serves a raise, the fundraising skill owns the raise — the ask, the terms, the investor conversation. This skill's plan is evidence of how money becomes customers, and it may be cited in that conversation; its hypotheses stay labeled as hypotheses (see the output contract), and no figure in the plan becomes a projection by being quoted.

- **Roads not taken.** Three popular sources were weighed for this build and left out, and the reasons are part of the method. A high-volume content gospel (Gary Vaynerchuk's) is grade (c) — advice resting on personal anecdote whose checkable claims fail the check: the "spend it all on social, nobody watches TV" position was answered with Nielsen data showing combined television and social campaigns outperforming either alone, and a doctrine of relentless volume states no condition under which volume stops working. A permission-and-remarkability gospel (Seth Godin's) supplies a principle, not evidence or method: consent before contact is kept in this skill, carried by consent law and deliverability practice, and on the exact ground of what makes people pass things on, the measured work (Berger and Milkman) is used instead of the manifesto. A loyalty gospel (Frederick Reichheld's, with the Net Promoter Score) is grade (c) dressed as grade (a): the retention-profit claim behind it has no source data anyone could find, and the score's claimed superiority as a growth predictor failed independent tests against ordinary satisfaction measures. A recommendation question may still be worth asking customers; it is not a growth law, and the After squares stand on Fader's models and Schmitt's referral data instead.

- **The plan is not the result.** This skill produces a stated system with stated checks. Whether the system works is decided by the checks running — the audits, the cohort measurement, the asset tests — and a plan that is never checked against its own numbers has the same standing as the folklore it replaced.
