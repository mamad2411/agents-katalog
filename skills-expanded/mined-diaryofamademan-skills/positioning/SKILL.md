> **How to read this**
>
> Positioning decides what position is owned. Its sibling skills work downstream of that decision. Comms owns public moments and how a thing is said — it says the position, it does not set it. Fundraising owns one concept inside a raise — it borrows a finished position, it does not set one. The one-page-marketing-plan skill plans around the position: its message square consumes this skill's output record directly. This skill's whole job is the decision those skills depend on, made once, on evidence, and written down in a form that holds.

## What this skill is

Ask a room of buyers what a company is for, and most companies get a shrug, a competitor's name, or a list of features. That is not a messaging problem. It is a position that was never decided — or was decided by default, by whoever shouted loudest in the category, until the company became the fourth name in someone else's frame.

Positioning is the decision about what a product will be thought of as, by whom, against what alternatives, and on what proof. This skill runs that decision as a procedure, in a fixed order, because the order is where the usual failures live. Companies pick a category first and then hunt for a difference that fits it. They list benefits nobody can check. They name a target customer so wide it refuses no one. Each of those is a step done out of order or a step skipped, and the procedure below is built to make each one visible.

Three habits do the work. The alternatives come from buyers, not from the company's own competitor list — what customers would actually do instead, including nothing. Every value claim carries proof a buyer can check, or it does not enter the record. And no position ships until it names its trade-offs: the customers it will not serve and the things it will not do. A position with no sacrifices is copywriting, and this skill's gate is built to catch exactly that.

## The objective

The goal is not a cleverer claim. Measured buying behavior — the Ehrenberg-Bass research program, decades of panel and single-source data across categories and countries — says brands grow by penetration: by being thought of, by more category buyers, in more buying situations, and being easy to buy when the moment comes. Perceived differentiation, on its own, is weak in that data and does not track loyalty or share the way positioning folklore assumes.

So the objective of a run of this skill is stated plainly: **a position that more category buyers can think of, in more buying situations, with proof attached.** Difference still matters — in considered purchases, where buyers compare and choose deliberately, win-and-loss evidence shows real differences deciding deals — and Step 3 is where difference is proved for exactly those buyers. But the position is built to be remembered and retrieved, not merely to be distinct. Every step is read against that objective before the record ships.

## The framing axiom

One idea frames the whole skill, labeled for what it is. Ries and Trout's founding insight (1981): **a position lives in the prospect's mind.** You do not get to write on a blank slate; you work with the associations buyers already hold, and focus beats breadth — one clear idea is retrievable, five good ideas are none. Later memory research supports the insight better than the original books supported it themselves. What does not survive is the books' status as law: they cite no empirical studies, their examples are cherry-picked winners, and specific claims (that being first wins, for one) have failed against the data — pioneers fail about half the time, and eventual leaders typically enter years after them. So the axiom is used as framing, never as rules. No claim from those books enters this procedure as a step unless the buying evidence carries it.

## A second frame — Schwartz's diagnosis, diagnostic half only

Eugene Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) contributes a diagnosis, and only a diagnosis. Before a position is stated, two things about the audience are named:

- **The best-fit customer's awareness stage** — one of five. Unaware: does not know the problem exists. Problem Aware: knows the problem, not the solutions. Solution Aware: knows solutions exist, not this product. Product Aware: knows this product, has not bought. Most Aware: knows the product and its proof, and needs the final reason.

- **The market's sophistication stage** — how many similar claims the market has already heard, one to five. At the early stages a plain claim is heard and believed. By the late stages every competitor has made the outcome claim and enlarged it, and buyers have learned to discount all of it.

The rule the two stages carry: **the owned concept's lead form must match them, and the record says how.** A concept led at the wrong awareness stage talks past its buyers — proof offered to a buyer who does not yet know the problem, a problem story told to a buyer who is already comparing products. And at high sophistication, stages four and five, the concept leads with the mechanism, never a bigger claim: when every rival claims the better outcome, the lead that still carries weight is the checkable one — the mechanism a buyer can inspect, working in the open. One of Schwartz's points is also cited as framing where it supports Step 7's entry-points work: desire is channeled, never created. A position attaches to a want that already exists, in a situation where it is already felt; it does not manufacture the want.

What stays out is the book's other half: the intensification craft — enlarging the promise, dramatizing it, stacking promises until the claim outruns its proof. That craft conflicts with this skill's claims discipline (Guardrail 2: no proof, no position), and it does not enter the procedure in any form.

Schwartz is labeled the way Ries and Trout are labeled above: a practitioner frame, not measured law. His stages were drawn from direct-response practice, not from panel data. Wherever the two stages disagree with buying evidence — win/loss accounts, buyer interviews, the run's own measures — the evidence overrules the frame. The diagnosis earns its place because it is checkable inside a run: awareness shows in what buyers say unprompted, and sophistication can be counted in the claims the Step 1 alternatives are already making.

## When to use

- **Before a launch** — a product entering a market where buyers already have a frame, an incumbent, or a habit it must displace.

- **A repositioning** — the market's current answer about the company is wrong, borrowed, or stuck at a stage the company has outgrown.

- **A new segment or a new market** — the existing position was built for buyers who are not the buyers in front of the company now.

- **When the pitch keeps changing** — sales, the site, and the deck each telling a different story is the symptom of a position that was never decided once.

- **When growth stalls at the same ceiling** — the company is known to the buyers who already buy, and invisible in the buying situations where the rest of the category is chosen.

## When not to use

- **Not to write copy.** This skill decides what position is owned. Headlines, launch posts, and campaign lines are comms' work, downstream of the record this skill returns.

- **Not to set a pitch.** A raise borrows the finished position. If a deck needs a different concept than the market position, that is a warning about one of them, and it is resolved here or in the market evidence — never by quietly editing the position for the room.

- **Not as a naming or identity exercise.** Names, logos, and visual identity carry a position; they do not decide one. Distinctive assets are audited in Step 7, after the position exists.

- **Not without buyer evidence.** If nobody can say what customers would do instead, what they love, and why deals were won and lost, the first job is gathering that evidence — interviews, win/loss calls, sales-call recordings. A position built from the team's opinions about buyers is a guess wearing the procedure's clothes.

## The procedure

### Before the steps — the evidence base

Dunford's components run on customer evidence, and a run starts by assembling it: recent win and loss accounts, in buyers' own words where possible; what customers say they would have done instead; the language happy customers use for what the product does for them; the deals lost to doing nothing. Where the evidence is thin, the run says so at the step it thins, and the record carries the gap. The components are worked in the order below, and the order is load-bearing: each step's output is the next step's input, and the category — the step most teams want to do first — is deliberately last.

### Step 1 — Competitive alternatives

**Question: if this product did not exist, what would the best-fit buyers actually do?**

List what buyers really reach for, in buyers' terms, not the company's competitive set. The list almost always includes the alternatives companies forget:

- **The status quo** — doing nothing, living with the problem, the workaround already in place. In many markets this is the largest competitor by far.

- **Manual and makeshift routes** — spreadsheets, email threads, an assistant, a general-purpose tool bent to the job.

- **Building or hiring it themselves** — an internal team, a contractor, an agency.

- **Direct competitors** — the named products buyers actually evaluated, which is a shorter and different list than every company in the category.

The alternatives are the frame everything else is measured against: an attribute is only unique against this list, and value is only value compared to one of these. Get this step from buyer evidence — sales calls and win/loss interviews — because teams reliably overstate direct competitors and understate doing nothing. One more question belongs here, because competitors react: **if we claim the position this run is building toward, who on this list can credibly claim it back, and how fast?** An alternative that can copy the claim in a quarter is a different planning problem than one that would have to rebuild to do it.

### Step 2 — Unique attributes

**Question: what does this product have, or do, that the alternatives do not?**

Attributes are facts about the product — capabilities, features, architecture, assets, terms — stated plainly enough to be checked. Not benefits, not outcomes, not adjectives. "Built on the customer's own data warehouse" is an attribute. "Insightful" is not.

Work attribute by attribute against the Step 1 list: for each candidate, name which alternatives lack it. An attribute every alternative also has is not discarded — it is filed for the gate, where it will be tested as a possible point of parity — but it does not enter this step's list. The output is a short list of attributes that are true, checkable, and actually unevenly distributed across the frame. If the list comes out empty, the run stops and says so: there is no position to build yet, and the honest outputs are a product gap or a parity position competed on availability, not a difference invented at this desk.

### Step 3 — Value, with proof

**Question: what do those attributes make possible for buyers — and what is the proof?**

Translate each unique attribute into the value it produces: the outcome a buyer gets that the alternatives do not deliver, in the buyer's terms. Then attach the proof. Proof is anything a buyer could in principle check: a measured result from real use, a named customer's account, a demonstration the buyer can run, a number with its method visible. A value claim with no proof is logged as a claim, in a separate list, and it does not enter the positioning record. Claims graduate to the record when their proof exists; until then they are a research backlog, not a position.

This is also the step where the differentiation question gets scoped honestly, because the evidence differs by buying type:

- **Considered purchases** — deliberate, compared, often expensive or risky. Here difference decides: buyers shortlist, evaluate, and pick for reasons, and win/loss evidence shows proved differences winning those deals. The value-and-proof list is built for these buyers first.

- **Repeat and low-deliberation purchases** — habitual, light-touch, memory-driven. Here the Ehrenberg-Bass data governs: buyers buy what they think of and can get, and fine differences in claims barely move them. The position still needs its proved value for the moments of comparison, but growth comes from Step 7's memory work, not from sharpening claims.

A run names which buying type it is primarily in. Most business products are the first; most consumer repeat purchases are the second; many are both, for different buyers — and the record says which, per buyer group, rather than pretending one logic covers all of it.

### Step 4 — Best-fit customers

**Question: who cares most about that value — and who demonstrably does?**

Best-fit customers are identified by characteristics, not by industry label alone: the traits shared by the customers who already love the product, buy fast, stay, and get the proved value most strongly. Dunford's test is retrospective before it is prospective — look at who the value has already landed with, and describe what they have in common: their situation, their constraint, the moment the problem bites, what they tried before.

Two disciplines ride on this step:

- **Refuse the everybody trap.** A best-fit description that excludes no one is not a segment; it is a hope. The description must be narrow enough that a reader could name companies or people who are *not* in it.

- **One beachhead first (Moore).** The record names one first segment — narrow enough to dominate fully, with the whole product that segment needs (Step 6 tests this) — and treats adjacent segments as later moves, radiating from references in the beachhead. Narrow here is a sequence, not a ceiling: the position is a spearhead, and breadth is built in memory (Step 7) and in later segments, not by widening the first target until it holds nothing.

The owned concept is drafted at this step, in the customers' own words from the evidence — the one idea the position claims, in the language buyers already use for the value. If the team has to invent vocabulary for it, the concept is not yet in the buyers' minds, and the framing axiom says that is where it has to live.

**Diagnose the two stages here.** With the best-fit customers named, the run records Schwartz's two stages (see "A second frame" above). The awareness stage is read from the same evidence as the segment itself: what these buyers say unprompted about the problem and the alternatives is the stage, stated in buyers' terms. The sophistication stage is counted against the Step 1 frame: how many of the alternatives are already making this category's outcome claim, and in what enlarged forms. Both stages then discipline the concept drafted above. Its lead form — what it puts first — must match them, and the record states the match. At sophistication stages four and five, the concept leads with the checkable mechanism, never with a larger version of the outcome claim.

A field note, from a run of this skill: in a market where every competitor claimed better results, the concept that landed led with checkability itself — the published record as the mechanism, open for any buyer to inspect. In the same run, a results-superlative claim was tested and failed. Read plainly: a stage-two claim, in a stage-five market.

### Step 5 — Market category

**Question: what frame, put around this value for these buyers, makes the value obvious?**

The category is chosen last, on purpose. A category is a frame: it tells buyers what to compare the product against, what to expect it to do, and what a fair price looks like. Chosen first, it dictates the attributes and the value before either is known. Chosen last, it is picked because it makes the proved value easiest to see.

The default is an existing category, or a named subcategory of one, because buyers already know how to buy it. Creating a new category is the rare exception, allowed only with evidence that no existing frame makes the value obvious — a new category must be explained before it can sell, and the explanation budget is real. The popular claim that category creators take most of a category's value rests on winners picked after the fact; it is not evidence, and it is not a reason to pay the explanation cost.

Two checks close the step:

- **Who does this frame advantage?** A category choice is a move in a contested game. Naming a frame educates the market for everyone in it — including the leader the frame was built to escape. If the chosen frame mostly advantages an incumbent who can outspend the education, say so in the record and weigh a narrower subcategory frame instead.

- **The trend (optional sixth component).** If a live trend — a technology shift, a regulation, a change in how buyers work — makes the value more urgent now, it may be attached to the record as the reason the moment is right. A trend is a tailwind, never the position itself: when the trend passes, the position must still stand.


### Step 6 — The strategy gate

**No position ships without passing this gate.** The full gate is in `references/the-strategy-gate.md`; its tests are these.

- **Trade-offs are named (Porter).** A position is a unique and valuable set of activities held together by fit — and it only exists if there are trade-offs. The record must name: the customers the company will not serve, the features or services it will not offer, and the deals it will walk away from. If nothing is refused, there is no position yet — there is a description of the product, and the run goes back to Step 4.

- **Activity fit is shown.** The named activities — product, pricing, service model, channels — must visibly reinforce the position and each other. Fit is also the defense: a rival can copy a claim in a quarter, but copying the position means copying the trade-offs and the activity system behind them, which costs them the customers their own position serves. A position whose activities contradict it (a premium position on a discount activity system) fails here.

- **Parity is separated from difference (Keller).** Every claimed difference from Steps 2–3 is tested against points of parity: what buyers treat as table stakes for the category — the things a product must have to be considered at all. A point of parity dressed up as a difference fails the gate; parity items are recorded as parity, because they still have to be delivered, and because selling table stakes as distinction teaches buyers to discount the real differences. What remains after the test is the position's actual points of difference, each with its proof from Step 3.

- **The whole product is present for the beachhead (Moore).** For the Step 4 beachhead segment, can those buyers actually get the promised value — product plus the services, integrations, onboarding, and proof they need to succeed with it? A position that promises value the beachhead cannot yet reach is a roadmap item, not a position.

- **The onlyness forcing exercise (optional, an exercise only).** Complete the sentence: "the only ___ that ___." It is a forcing question for focus, not a source and not a test — its answer is then run back through Steps 1–3 and the parity test, and it survives only if they carry it.

- **One warning, posted at the gate (Moon).** Competitive benchmarking breeds sameness: rivals who watch each other converge. Step 1 studies alternatives to find the frame — the gate checks that the run has not simply mirrored the frame's leader with better adjectives.

### Step 7 — Memory, assets, and measurement

A position that buyers cannot retrieve at the moment of buying does not exist in the only place positions live. This step attaches the position to memory, using Romaniuk's mechanics; the full method is in `references/memory-and-measurement.md`.

- **Category entry points.** List the buying situations the position must attach to: the cues — a trigger event, a place, a time, a feeling, a task gone wrong — that start a buyer thinking about the category. The owned concept is then stated so it can be retrieved at those points, and the list goes into the record, because it is the checklist all later memory-building work is judged against. Breadth lives here: the position stays narrow (Steps 4–5), while the entry-point list is as wide as the category's real buying situations. Schwartz's framing point supports the list's discipline: desire is channeled, never created, so the entry points are situations where the want already exists — the position attaches to them rather than trying to manufacture them.

- **The distinctive-asset audit.** The brand's assets — name, marks, phrases, formats, faces, sounds — are scored on two measures across category buyers: **Fame** (how many buyers link the asset to the brand) and **Uniqueness** (how exclusively they link it to this brand and no other). An asset with high fame and high uniqueness can carry the position into memory. An asset buyers link to a competitor is worse than no asset. The audit names which assets carry the position and which are retired or rebuilt.

- **Measures for whether it is landing.** The record closes with the measures that will be read later, chosen at this step, not improvised after: retrieval of the brand at the named entry points among category buyers; the owned concept's association with the brand versus alternatives; asset Fame and Uniqueness movement; penetration within the beachhead segment. A position that cannot state how its landing would be measured is unfinished — the gate sends it back.

### Step 8 — The statement and the record

The run ends by assembling the positioning record (the output contract below) and running Moore's statement template over it as a completeness check:

> For (best-fit segment) who (the compelling reason to buy), (product) is a (market category) that (the key proved benefit). Unlike (the named alternative), (product) (the key difference).

If any slot cannot be filled from the steps above — no named alternative, no proved benefit, a segment too wide to name — the position is not finished, and the empty slot names the step to redo. The statement is a check, not the deliverable: the record is the deliverable. One last test on the statement, from the comms discipline: **say it out loud.** If it cannot be said in one breath, in words a buyer uses, it fails — positions are carried by people repeating them, and nobody repeats a sentence they cannot say.

## The tensions, resolved

The sources behind this skill disagree in four places. The resolutions are part of the method, not footnotes.

- **Differentiation versus availability.** Difference decides considered purchases; memory and availability drive repeat ones. Resolution: scope by buying type (Step 3). Difference is the argument proved to best-fit buyers; availability is the growth engine. Neither cancels the other. A note on the counter-data: some brand datasets find perceived difference the biggest factor in predisposition and price performance. The camps measure different things — predisposition versus actual buying — so this skill treats perceived difference as valuable where it is measured in buyers, and never as a substitute for being thought of at all.

- **Category: create, choose, or enter.** Creation is the rare, expensive exception; the frame is chosen last, usually an existing one, and a niche inside a real market is dominated first (Steps 4–5).

- **Trade-offs versus value innovation.** The claim that a firm can win on difference and low cost together rests on a dataset that was never shown. The trade-off claim can be audited in a firm's own activities. This skill sides with trade-offs (Step 6).

- **Focus versus reach.** Narrow the position and the first segment; go broad in memory across category entry points (Step 7). The position is a spearhead, not a fence.

## Guardrails

1. **Alternatives come from buyers.** The competitive frame is what buyers would actually do — status quo included — taken from win/loss and sales evidence, never from the company's own list of companies it dislikes.

2. **No proof, no position.** A value claim enters the record only with checkable proof attached. Unproved value is logged as a claim and worked as a backlog; it is never positioned on.

3. **Category last.** The category is derived from attributes, value, and best-fit customers — in that order. A run that starts by naming a category is restarted.

4. **No trade-offs, no ship.** A record with an empty trade-offs field fails the gate and does not ship, whatever its other merits.

5. **Parity is not difference.** Table stakes are delivered and recorded as parity. They are never sold as the distinction.

6. **One beachhead first.** The record names one first segment. Widening the target to keep everyone happy voids the run.

7. **Framing is not evidence.** The mind-first axiom frames the work. No author's "law" is a rule in this procedure unless the buying evidence carries it.

8. **One position, for the market.** The record holds a single position. Other skills borrow it whole: comms says it, fundraising pitches with it, the plan plans around it. Adapting the position per audience — a bigger category for investors, a softer claim for a partner — is out of bounds; emphasis may vary, the position may not.

9. **Positions are set on evidence, and re-run on evidence.** A position changes when buyer evidence changes — new alternatives, a proved or disproved claim, a segment that stops fitting — through a new run of this skill, not by drift.

## Calls on sibling skills

This skill does not run alone; the playbook is one playbook, and a skill calls the siblings its work needs, by name, at the step that needs them. The calls are steps, not suggestions:

- **At the evidence step, call first-principles-thinking** — the category is decomposed to what buyers actually pay for and what the product provably does, before any frame is chosen.

- **At the statement step, call comms** — the position is written to be repeated by other people: short enough to survive retelling, plain enough to survive a tired reader.

- **On completion, hand off to one-page-marketing-plan** — the adopted position is that skill's input; the plan is where the position becomes channels and cadence.

## Output contract

Every run returns a **positioning record**, in this format. The first block is the chain-out block: it is written to be consumed directly as the message square (square 2) of the one-page-marketing-plan skill, with no re-derivation.

```text
POSITIONING RECORD

— Chain-out block (for one-page-marketing-plan, square 2 — message) —
Owned concept:        <the one idea the position claims, in buyers' own words>
Market category:      <the frame chosen last; an existing category or named subcategory, unless the new-category evidence test was passed and is cited>
Competitive frame:    <the named alternatives buyers would choose instead, status quo first if it is the largest>
Best-fit customers:   <the characteristics of the buyers who care most, narrow enough to exclude>
Trade-offs:           <customers not served, things not offered, deals walked away from>

— Supporting record —
Beachhead:            <the one first segment, dominated before any other>
Customer awareness stage: <the best-fit customer's Schwartz stage, in buyers' terms — what they already know and believe, from the evidence>
Market sophistication stage: <1–5 — how many similar claims the market has already heard, and how the owned concept therefore leads; at stages 4–5, the checkable mechanism first, never a bigger claim>
Unique attributes:    <the checked attributes from Step 2, each against the frame>
Value, with proof:    <each value claim, paired with its checkable proof>
Unproved claims:      <claims logged without proof — the research backlog, none positioned on>
Points of parity:     <table stakes, recorded as parity>
Points of difference: <what survived the gate's parity test, with proof>
Category entry points: <the buying situations the position must attach to>
Trend (optional):     <the tailwind, if one was attached at Step 5>
Distinctive assets:   <assets carrying the position, with Fame × Uniqueness standing; assets retired>
Positioning statement: <Moore template, completed — the completeness check>
Measures:             <how landing will be read: retrieval at entry points, concept association, asset movement, beachhead penetration>
Evidence gaps:        <where the buyer evidence was thin, and what would close it>
```

**Then run the writing pass. This step calls two sibling skills. It is a procedure, not a style reminder.** When a positioning record ships in raw strategy register, the owned concept reads as internal jargon and the record stops sounding like the buyers it is meant to win.

1. **Humanizer.** Run the work-humanizer skill in rewrite mode over the positioning record, including the chain-out block and the supporting record. Facts, figures, quotes, names, dates, and statuses never change in the pass. What changes is the register.

2. **Plain style.** Apply the writing-styles skill's plain rules to the result: short sentences, one idea each, numbers spoken the way a person says them, bold only on the few facts that matter.

3. **The gate.** The positioning record is not done until the pass has run on the exact text being delivered.

Buyer quotes, proof figures, and the statement stay exact — they are evidence. The owned concept is written in the buyers' vocabulary, not the company's internal terms; if a phrase needs a glossary, it is not the concept. Write bulleted lists loose, a blank line between items, so each point stands on its own.

## Limits and boundaries

- **Boundary — comms.** Comms owns public moments and how a thing is said: the launch post, the campaign, the words at the moment of contact. Positioning decides what position is owned, upstream of all of that. If a piece of copy needs a different position to work, the finding goes back to a positioning run; copy does not quietly reposition the company.

- **Boundary — fundraising.** Fundraising owns one concept inside a raise. It borrows the finished position from the record and adapts emphasis for the room; it does not set, widen, or soften the position. Investor pressure for a bigger category or a different concept is market evidence to be tested by a new run of this skill — never a deck edit that becomes the position by repetition.

- **Boundary — the plan.** The one-page-marketing-plan skill consumes the chain-out block as its message square. If the plan's other squares (media, lead handling, delivery) reveal the position cannot be carried — the assets fail the audit, the entry points cannot be reached with the available media — that is evidence for a re-run, recorded as such.

- **The evidence base is uneven, and the skill says so.** The procedure's method core is a practice framework, however battle-tested; the buying-behavior layer is measured and replicated; the framing axiom is an insight, not a finding. Where a run leans on the weaker layers, the record's evidence-gaps field is where that is admitted.

- **Roads not taken, named once.** Category-creation programs built on "category kings take most of the value" were not used as a foundation: the underlying sample was picked after outcomes were known, and the figure was never independently tested. Blue-ocean value innovation was not used: its dataset was never shown, its new-market advantage proved temporary in the one independent test, and its core move contradicts the trade-off gate without answering it. A company-DNA typology (mother, mechanic, missionary) was not used: its types are unfalsifiable and its evidence is anecdote. Each may be an interesting read; none carries weight in the procedure.

- **A position is not a result.** This skill decides and records. Whether the position lands is read in Step 7's measures over time, by the work that carries it — product, media, sales — which this skill does not do.
