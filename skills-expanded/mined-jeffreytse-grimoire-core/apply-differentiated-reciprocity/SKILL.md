# Apply Differentiated Reciprocity

Respond to a wrong with proportionate fairness — neither indiscriminate forgiveness that erodes accountability nor escalating retaliation that compounds the conflict — and respond to genuine kindness with kindness in kind, rather than applying the same response regardless of how you were actually treated.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Origin:** In the Analects (論語, Book 14, "Xian Wen"), a questioner asks Confucius about the popular notion of repaying a wrong with kindness (以德報怨) — essentially unconditional forgiveness toward those who wrong you. Confucius rejects this directly, asking: if a wrong is repaid with kindness, how then would genuine kindness itself be repaid? His stated position is a differentiated response: repay a wrong with 直 — proportionate directness, fairness, an undistorted response that is neither excessively lenient nor excessively harsh — and repay kindness with kindness (以德報德), reciprocating good treatment in kind. The teaching's structure is a deliberate rejection of two failure modes: indiscriminate leniency, which erodes the accountability that makes fair dealing sustainable, and (by the same logic) indiscriminate retaliation, which escalates conflict rather than resolving it.

**Adopted by:** Robert Axelrod's iterated-tournament research in *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984) — a foundational, widely taught result in game theory — found that reciprocity-based strategies (respond to cooperation with cooperation, respond to defection with a proportionate, non-escalating response) systematically outperformed both unconditionally cooperative strategies, which are exploited by bad-faith actors, and unconditionally hostile strategies, which forfeit the sustained gains available from mutual cooperation. Nowak and Sigmund's later refinement (*Nature*, 1992/1993) found that strategies combining bounded forgiveness with continued responsiveness to a genuine pattern of wrongdoing ("generous tit-for-tat") are more stable over repeated interaction than strict, unforgiving retaliation.

**Impact:** Axelrod's tournament results are a specific, falsifiable, replicated finding, not an abstract claim: across repeated computational tournaments, the strategies that survived and outperformed were specifically those that reciprocated in kind rather than defaulting to either extreme — confirming, in a modern and quantitative setting, the same three-way structure Confucius's answer describes: neither unconditional appeasement nor unconditional retaliation, but a response calibrated to what was actually received.

**Why best:** This is a different mechanism than the closest adjacent skills already in this repo. `apply-non-transactional-giving` is about proactively giving without expecting return, to build long-term social capital — a unilateral generosity strategy, not a response to how you were actually treated. `apply-consequence-clarity` addresses institutional and behavioral-system design (clarity, certainty, swiftness, proportionality of consequences generally) — a systems-design framework, not a personal ethical response to being wronged or treated well. `apply-institutional-integrity` is about consistent rule enforcement regardless of relationship — a different mechanism (uniform application of an existing rule) from calibrating your own response differently depending on whether you were wronged or treated well.

## Steps

1. **Determine which category the situation actually falls into.** Before responding, classify whether you were genuinely wronged, genuinely treated well, or neither — the appropriate response differs by category, and applying one uniform response regardless of category is exactly the failure mode this principle rejects.

2. **For a wrong, respond with proportionate fairness, not indiscriminate forgiveness.** Don't default to excusing or absorbing the wrong without consequence simply to avoid conflict — unconditional leniency toward wrongdoing erodes the accountability that makes fair dealing sustainable for everyone else in the relationship or system.

3. **For a wrong, respond with proportionate fairness, not escalating retaliation.** The response to a wrong should be direct and fair — addressing the actual wrong done — not amplified into a punishment disproportionate to what occurred, which compounds the conflict rather than resolving it.

4. **For genuine kindness, reciprocate in kind.** When someone has genuinely treated you well, respond with kindness in return — this is a distinct category from the wrong-response calibration above, and treating good-faith kindness the same way you'd treat a wrong (with guarded, minimal, proportionate response) forfeits the relationship-building value that reciprocated kindness provides.

5. **Recalibrate as the pattern becomes clear, rather than reacting to a single instance in isolation.** A single ambiguous act may not clearly indicate a wrong or kindness — allow the response to be informed by an emerging pattern rather than overreacting to one instance, while still responding proportionately to what has actually occurred so far.

## Rules

- Classify the situation (wronged, treated well, or neither) before choosing a response — the same response applied regardless of category is the specific failure this principle rejects.
- Never respond to a wrong with unconditional forgiveness that has no accountability component — this erodes fair dealing for everyone else in the relationship or system, not just the immediate situation.
- Never respond to a wrong with retaliation disproportionate to what occurred — escalation compounds conflict rather than resolving it.
- Reciprocate genuine kindness with kindness — this is a distinct response category from wrong-calibration, not a weaker version of the same caution.

## Examples

**Trigger:** A business partner breaches a minor term of an agreement, and the instinct is either to let it go entirely to preserve the relationship, or to respond with a maximal, disproportionate consequence.
→ Respond with proportionate fairness instead of either extreme: address the specific breach directly and fairly — a clear, appropriately-scaled consequence or correction — without excusing it entirely (which would erode the agreement's enforceability going forward) and without escalating to a disproportionate response that damages the relationship beyond what the actual breach warrants.

**Trigger:** A colleague goes out of their way to genuinely help on a project with no expectation of anything in return.
→ Reciprocate the kindness in kind — this is the distinct "treated well" category, not the "wronged" category, and the appropriate response is direct reciprocal kindness, not a cautious, minimal, proportionate-to-a-wrong style response that would be miscalibrated for what actually happened.

## Common Mistakes

- **Applying the same cautious, minimal response to both wrongs and kindness.** These are genuinely different categories requiring different responses — treating good-faith kindness the way you'd treat a wrong forfeits the relationship value of reciprocation.
- **Defaulting to unconditional forgiveness of wrongs to avoid conflict.** This erodes the accountability that sustains fair dealing, even when it feels like the more generous or peaceful choice in the moment.
- **Escalating the response to a wrong beyond what's proportionate.** Retaliation disproportionate to the actual wrong compounds conflict rather than resolving it, and forfeits the gains available from eventually restoring cooperative dealing.
- **Reacting to a single ambiguous instance as if the pattern were already clear.** Overreacting to one unclear instance, in either direction, can miscalibrate the relationship before enough information exists to classify it accurately.

## When NOT to Use

- When there is no genuine wrong or kindness to classify — routine, neutral interactions don't need this differentiated-response framework applied to them.
- In contexts governed by formal rules or contracts that already specify the required response to a breach — use the applicable formal process; this principle applies to personal/relational judgment calls, not to situations where the response is already contractually or legally determined.
- When safety is at risk — a proportionate-fairness response is not appropriate when the situation requires immediate protective action rather than a calibrated relational response.
