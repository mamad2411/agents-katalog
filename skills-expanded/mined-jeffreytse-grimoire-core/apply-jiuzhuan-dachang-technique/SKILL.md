# Apply Jiuzhuan Dachang Technique

Fully deodorize a strong-smelling offal cut through thorough cleaning and a purging blanch, cook it through a sequential boil-then-fry-then-braise process, and build the braising sauce so a diner experiences distinct flavor notes in order — sweet, then sour, then spicy, then salty, then an aromatic finish — rather than one blended taste throughout.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Adopted by:** Jiuzhuan Dachang is a signature dish of Lu cuisine, one of China's officially recognized Eight Culinary Traditions, taught in Chinese culinary academy curricula as a demonstration piece for offal-preparation and multi-stage-cooking mastery. Lu cuisine's techniques, developed and refined in Jinan and the broader Shandong region, historically shaped much of imperial Qing court cuisine and remain foundational teaching material in Chinese professional culinary training.

**Impact:** Pork large intestine, prepared without thorough deodorizing, retains a strong ammonia-like odor that most diners find unpalatable regardless of how skillfully the rest of the dish is executed — the deodorizing prep is the precondition that makes the cut usable at all, not a garnish step. The dish's defining sequential five-flavor sauce is documented specifically because it produces a different sensory result than a blended sauce: diners report tasting each flavor note distinctly as it arrives, rather than a single averaged flavor, which is the whole point of building the reduction in a specific order rather than mixing all five flavor components together at once.

**Why best:** This differs from generic technique in two specific ways. `apply-braising-technique` is a single-method (sear→braise) process that explicitly avoids boiling during cooking; it has no framework for a cut that specifically needs a purging boil stage before frying and braising, and no offal-deodorizing step at all. `design-flavor-pairing` addresses which tastes a dish should contain, not the discipline of engineering a sauce so those tastes arrive in a deliberate sequence rather than simultaneously. Both the offal-deodorizing prep and the sequential (not simultaneous) flavor construction are the dish's actual defining techniques, and neither is covered by a generic braising or flavor-pairing approach.

## Steps

1. **Clean the intestine thoroughly before any cooking begins.** Turn the intestine inside out and scrub both sides — the strong odor originates largely from residue on the inner lining. Use an abrasive method (coarse salt, flour, or a vinegar rinse) to strip mucus and residue rather than relying on rinsing with water alone, which doesn't remove it.

2. **Blanch to purge remaining odor before the intestine is cooked further.** A boiling-water blanch, sometimes with ginger, scallion, or wine added to the water, draws out ammonia-smelling compounds that scrubbing alone doesn't fully remove. This purging step is a precondition for every later step — skipping it means no amount of sauce can mask the underlying smell.

3. **Boil to tenderize before frying.** After the purging blanch, cook the intestine in a simmering liquid (often with aromatics) until tender enough to eat but not yet cooked through to the final texture — this stage builds tenderness that deep-frying alone, applied to raw intestine, would not achieve without excessive cooking time.

4. **Deep-fry to crisp the exterior and render remaining fat.** Cut the boiled intestine into sections and deep-fry until the exterior is crisp and browned. This stage renders out fat the boiling stage didn't remove and creates a textural contrast (crisp exterior, tender interior) that a single braise-only method can't produce.

5. **Build the sauce to deliver five flavors in sequence, not blended together.** Construct the braising sauce/reduction so a diner tastes sweet first, then sour, then spicy/hot, then salty, then a final aromatic note (commonly from five-spice or a similar aromatic) — sequencing the components (typically sugar caramelized first, then vinegar, then chili, then soy/salt-based seasoning, with the aromatic finish added last or bloomed separately) rather than combining all five at once into one averaged flavor.

6. **Braise the fried intestine in the sequenced sauce to finish.** Return the fried intestine to the pan with the sauce and reduce until the sauce coats the pieces and the flavors have set into the exterior, without cooking so long that the crisp texture built in the frying stage is fully lost.

## Rules

- Never skip the deodorizing blanch — scrubbing alone removes surface residue but doesn't purge the odor compounds a hot-water blanch draws out; skipping this step undermines every later step.
- Cook in the boil-then-fry-then-braise order, not any other sequence — boiling first builds tenderness the frying stage needs, frying next builds the textural contrast, and braising last sets the flavor without needing to also carry the tenderizing or texturing work.
- Build the five-flavor sauce in a deliberate sequence, not as a single mixed batch — the technique's entire distinguishing feature is sequential flavor arrival, not simultaneous blended flavor.

## Common Mistakes

- **Relying on rinsing with water instead of an abrasive clean plus a purging blanch.** Water alone does not remove the residue and odor compounds responsible for the smell; both an abrasive scrub and a hot-water purge are necessary.
- **Skipping the boiling stage and going straight to frying raw intestine.** This produces an undesirable texture — either undercooked and tough, or requiring so much frying time that the exterior burns before the interior is properly cooked.
- **Mixing all five sauce components together at once rather than sequencing them.** This produces one blended flavor rather than the dish's defining sequential-tasting experience, which is the actual point of the technique.
- **Over-braising after frying until the crisp exterior is fully lost.** The final braise should set the sauce and flavor without fully dissolving the textural contrast the frying stage built.

## When NOT to Use

- When the goal is a quick, single-stage preparation — this technique's three sequential cooking stages plus a deodorizing prep are specifically suited to a difficult offal cut and a dish meant to showcase sequential-flavor construction, not a fast weeknight method.
- For cuts or ingredients without a strong-odor problem to solve — the deodorizing steps are unnecessary overhead for ingredients that don't have the specific odor issue offal cuts present.
- When a single, blended flavor profile is the actual goal — if the dish doesn't call for a diner to taste distinct sequential notes, a standard combined sauce is simpler and more appropriate.
