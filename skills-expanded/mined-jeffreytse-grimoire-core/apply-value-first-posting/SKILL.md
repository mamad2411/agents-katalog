# Apply Value-First Posting

Post rarely on a personal feed reaching distant contacts, and make every post something that gives the viewer real value — a specific insight, a distilled takeaway, a novel observation — rather than a personal-life update or generic greeting, because low-value, frequent posts read as noise to anyone who isn't already close to you, while distant or senior contacts register your credibility only from content that is actually useful to them.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** A message that functions perfectly well to maintain a close relationship (a holiday greeting, a personal-life update) carries essentially zero information for a distant contact, because a genuinely close relationship doesn't require it — the people who actually know you well don't need a scheduled greeting to stay connected, and the people who don't know you well can't extract any signal about your competence or judgment from it. Distant contacts and senior figures, who receive far more incoming content than they can attend to, filter almost entirely on whether a piece of content is useful to them specifically — not on whether it demonstrates ongoing personal familiarity. This reframes a personal feed from a relationship-maintenance channel (which it is poorly suited for, at the frequency generic greetings require) into a low-frequency, high-signal credibility-broadcasting channel — a fundamentally different use requiring a different posting discipline.

**Austin Kleon, "Show Your Work!" (2014):** Kleon's central argument is that sharing your process, your discoveries, and your genuine points of view publicly — rather than only finished, polished self-promotion or unrelated personal content — is what makes someone findable and interesting to people who don't already know them, because it gives an outside observer something concrete and specific to evaluate and respond to. The book explicitly frames this as a deliberate practice of curation: sharing selectively and specifically, not broadcasting everything that happens in one's day.

**Gary Vaynerchuk, "Jab, Jab, Jab, Right Hook" (2013):** Vaynerchuk's framework — give value repeatedly ("jabs") before ever making an ask ("right hook") — established a widely adopted content-sequencing principle in content marketing: an audience's tolerance for eventually being asked for something is a direct function of how much genuine value they've received first, and value has to be judged from the audience's perspective (does this help or inform them), not the poster's perspective (is this true or notable to me).

**Dorie Clark, "Stand Out" (2015):** Clark's research and case studies on professional reputation-building document that individuals who build a following and professional credibility through public content consistently do so by sharing a distilled, original point of view on their area of expertise — a specific take on a book, an analysis of an industry development, an original observation — rather than generic updates, and that this content is deliberately infrequent and curated rather than a continuous stream of unfiltered activity.

**Steve Woods, "Digital Body Language" (2009):** Woods, co-founder of the marketing-automation company Eloqua, formalized the concept that a person's online engagement behavior — what they view, click, and react to — functions as a readable signal of genuine interest, distinct from and often more reliable than what they say directly. This principle underlies "social selling," a methodology formalized and taught by sales practitioners including Koka Sexton and Jill Rowley and adopted as a structured practice (via tools like LinkedIn's Sales Navigator and Social Selling Index): using a specific prospect's own engagement with content — a like, a view, a reaction — as the justified, low-friction opening for direct outreach on that specific topic, rather than cold, context-free contact.

**Content Marketing Institute, "B2B Content Marketing Benchmarks, Budgets, and Trends" (annual):** CMI's long-running annual research on B2B content consumption consistently finds senior decision-makers and executives disproportionately consume long-form formats — newsletters, articles, in-depth written content — while short-form video is the format with the broadest reach among general consumer audiences. The practical implication is a format-to-audience matching decision distinct from the value-first content decision itself: the same underlying discipline (post rarely, post genuine value) still requires choosing the format and platform where the specific target audience actually spends attention, since even excellent value-first content in the wrong format for a given audience fails to reach them.

**Adopted by:** Content-marketing sequencing principles from "Jab, Jab, Jab, Right Hook" are widely taught in social media marketing training and adopted broadly in brand and personal content strategy; Kleon's "share your work" framework and Clark's personal-branding research are both widely cited in professional-development and personal-branding coaching practice as the standard alternative to generic self-promotional or personal-life social content; social-selling methodology built on Woods' digital-body-language concept is formalized in LinkedIn's Sales Navigator and Social Selling Index, adopted broadly in B2B sales organizations; CMI's B2B content format research is standard reference material in B2B marketing and content strategy practice.
**Impact:** Clark's documented case studies of individuals who built professional followings and inbound opportunity through public content consistently show the pattern of low-frequency, high-value, expertise-specific posting rather than high-frequency personal updates; Vaynerchuk's value-first sequencing framework, widely adopted in content marketing practice, is credited with materially better audience receptiveness to an eventual direct ask, compared to content strategies that lead with self-promotion or lack a clear value-to-audience component; social-selling practice built on reading engagement signals is documented to produce higher-quality, better-timed outreach than context-free cold contact, precisely because the engagement itself confirms genuine interest in the specific topic before any direct approach is made; CMI's benchmarking research consistently finds format-audience mismatches (e.g., short-form-only strategies) under-reach senior decision-maker audiences relative to long-form and newsletter formats.

## Steps

1. **Before posting, ask whether this specific piece of content gives a viewer who barely knows you something useful or genuinely interesting — not whether it's true or notable to you personally.** A personal-life event (working late, a traffic jam, a routine greeting) is real and true but carries no transferable value to a distant viewer; screen every candidate post against the viewer's benefit, not your own experience of the moment.

2. **Default to silence over a low-value post.** Since posting frequency itself carries a cost (each low-value post trains distant viewers to tune you out, reducing the attention any future post receives), the bar for posting should be "this is worth someone's attention," not "something happened today."

3. **Draw content specifically from genuine engagement with new information — a book, a talk, an original observation others haven't already made — and distill it into a specific, concrete takeaway rather than a vague reaction.** "Read a great book" carries no value; a specific insight extracted and explained in your own words does.

4. **Favor being useful over being merely interesting or entertaining, when the two are in tension.** A post that teaches a distant viewer something they can apply is a stronger credibility signal than a post that is amusing or relatable but not actionable — utility, not entertainment value, is the primary filter.

5. **Keep the volume genuinely low — treat "few but excellent" as the actual constraint, not an aspiration.** A high-value post surrounded by frequent low-value ones is still read in the context of the low-value baseline; consistency of quality across a low-frequency feed is what builds the credibility signal over time.

6. **Distinguish this channel explicitly from direct interpersonal relationship maintenance.** A broadcast post to an entire network is not a substitute for genuine one-on-one relationship investment with the specific people who matter most — pair this with direct, individually-tailored engagement (see `apply-non-transactional-giving`) for the relationships that warrant it, rather than relying on broadcast posting alone.

7. **Treat a specific senior or distant contact's engagement with a specific post as a genuine, licensed interest signal — and use it as the opening for direct follow-up on that exact topic.** Senior figures and high-status people are frequent, high-attention consumers of social feeds precisely because it is often the only content they can attend to during otherwise unproductive time (meetings, waiting, travel); a like or reaction from a specific person on a specific post reveals real, current interest in that topic, and following up directly on that same topic is a natural continuation of an interaction they already initiated — not a cold, unprompted approach.

8. **Match the content format and platform to the specific audience you're trying to reach, not to whichever format is easiest to produce or most popular in general.** Per CMI's B2B research, short-form video reaches the broadest general consumer audience, while long-form written content and newsletter-style formats disproportionately reach senior decision-makers and entrepreneurs — the value-first discipline still applies within whichever format you choose, but the format itself must match where your specific target audience actually spends attention.

9. **Send connection requests freely — the cost of doing so is negligible — but treat being added by the other person as the stronger signal.** There is no real downside to sending a connection request; however, someone choosing to add you on their own initiative, typically because they found your content valuable, is a stronger indicator of genuine interest than a request you initiated, and warrants correspondingly more confidence in following up.

## Rules

- Screen every candidate post by whether it provides genuine value to someone who barely knows you, not by whether the content is true or personally significant to you.
- Default to not posting rather than posting something low-value — posting frequency itself carries a cost to how much attention future posts receive.
- Prioritize utility over entertainment value when choosing what to share; a post that teaches something applicable outperforms one that is merely relatable or amusing as a credibility signal to distant viewers.
- Treat broadcast posting as a distinct channel from direct interpersonal relationship maintenance — it complements, but does not replace, individually-tailored engagement with the people who matter most.
- Treat a specific person's engagement with a specific post as real interest data, and use it — not a cold, context-free message — as the basis for any direct follow-up with that person on that topic.
- Match content format to the specific audience's actual attention habits (long-form/newsletter for senior decision-makers, short-form for broad consumer reach) rather than defaulting to whichever format is most convenient or trending.

## Examples

**Professional visibility:** A mid-career professional stops posting daily personal-life updates and instead posts roughly once every few weeks, each time sharing a specific, distilled insight from a book relevant to their field or a concrete takeaway from an industry talk. Over time, a senior contact in their extended network — who would never have engaged with routine personal updates — begins reaching out directly in response to specific posts, leading to a substantive professional conversation neither party would have otherwise initiated.

**Distinguishing signal from noise:** Someone trying to stay visible to a former manager considers sending a generic holiday greeting message, then instead posts a specific, well-reasoned take on a recent development in their industry to their broader network. The former manager, who receives the holiday greeting from dozens of people and reads none of them closely, notices and responds specifically to the industry post because it offered something to evaluate.

**Value-first sequencing:** A consultant building a professional following posts a genuinely useful framework distilled from client work several times before ever posting anything self-promotional about their services. When they eventually do share a direct offer, the audience's receptiveness is measurably higher than it would have been without the preceding value-first posts, consistent with the "jab before right hook" sequencing principle.

**Engagement as licensed follow-up:** A senior executive, connected to a junior professional's broader network, likes a specific post the junior professional shared distilling an insight from an industry talk. The junior professional treats the like as confirmed, specific interest and sends a brief, directly related follow-up — a relevant article or a one-line additional thought on the same topic — rather than a generic introduction request; the executive, having already signaled interest in that exact subject, responds naturally to the topical follow-up in a way a cold message would not have prompted.

## Common Mistakes

- **Posting personal-life updates (traffic, overtime, routine greetings) under the belief that frequent activity itself maintains visibility.** This produces the opposite effect for distant contacts, training them to filter out future posts as noise.
- **Treating a broadcast post as sufficient relationship maintenance for people who actually matter most.** Broadcast content is a passive, low-cost credibility signal for a broad audience; it does not substitute for direct, individually-tailored engagement with key relationships.
- **Sharing that you engaged with something valuable (read a book, attended a talk) without distilling any specific, transferable insight from it.** "I read a great book" provides no value to the viewer; the specific takeaway is what makes the post worth their attention.
- **Prioritizing being interesting or entertaining over being useful, when building professional credibility with distant contacts is the actual goal.** Utility is the stronger, more reliable credibility signal for an audience that doesn't already know you well.

## When NOT to Use

- For content genuinely intended for close relationships that already have other, more direct channels of contact — this technique addresses the broad, distant-audience broadcast channel specifically, not communication with people you're already close to.
- When the platform or audience context is explicitly personal/social rather than professional (e.g., a private group chat with close friends) — applying value-first screening there misreads the actual purpose of that channel.
- When building a following or platform is not an actual goal — if the broadcast channel serves no professional-visibility purpose for you, the overhead of curating posts this deliberately isn't justified.
