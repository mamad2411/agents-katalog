# Apply Earnings Myopia Defense

Before letting quarterly-earnings pressure cut or delay a genuinely positive-long-term-value investment — R&D, infrastructure, maintenance — recognize this as a specific, well-documented, measurable cause of real value destruction, and build structural protections that keep long-term investment decisions insulated from short-term reporting pressure.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** This is not a vague complaint that "companies think too short-term" — Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal's research specifically measured how often financial executives would sacrifice a project with genuinely positive long-term value specifically to hit a quarterly earnings target, and found this trade-off is common, deliberate, and widely acknowledged by the executives making it, not an accidental byproduct of poor planning. Because the mechanism is specific and measurable (earnings-target pressure causing a deliberate cut to positive-NPV long-term investment), the defense is also specific: structurally reduce or eliminate the short-term reporting pressure that creates the incentive to make this trade-off, rather than relying on individual managers to resist it through willpower alone.

**Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal (2005):** This widely cited survey of over 400 financial executives found a striking, explicit result: a majority of surveyed CFOs stated they would decrease discretionary spending on R&D, advertising, and maintenance — even when they believed such spending had genuine long-term positive value — specifically to meet a quarterly earnings benchmark. The survey also found executives placed a high priority on meeting earnings benchmarks (matching the prior year's earnings, meeting analyst consensus, and reporting smooth, low-volatility earnings) even above maximizing the firm's actual long-term value, and were explicit that this reflected real, deliberate trade-offs rather than an oversight.

**Bushee (1998):** Provided empirical, firm-level evidence for the same mechanism from the opposite direction — measuring actual R&D spending against a firm's institutional ownership composition, Bushee found that firms with a higher proportion of "transient" institutional investors (those trading on short-term earnings performance rather than holding for long-term value) were significantly more likely to cut R&D spending specifically to avoid missing short-term earnings expectations, compared to otherwise similar firms with more long-term-oriented ownership — directly linking the composition of a company's investor base to the severity of this specific underinvestment pattern.

**Google's 2004 IPO letter ("An Owner's Manual") and Unilever's 2009 policy change:** Both companies made an explicit, structural change specifically to defend against this mechanism: declining to provide quarterly earnings guidance to analysts and investors. Google's founders stated directly in their IPO letter that they would not manage the company to hit short-term numbers and would not provide quarterly guidance, explicitly to protect long-term decision-making from short-term reporting pressure. Unilever's 2009 discontinuation of quarterly profit reporting, under then-CEO Paul Polman, was similarly framed as a deliberate structural change to allow the company to prioritize longer-term investment decisions without the recurring pressure of a quarterly public benchmark.

**Adopted by:** Both Google and Berkshire Hathaway have long-standing, well-documented policies of declining to provide quarterly earnings guidance specifically to protect long-term decision-making from short-term reporting pressure; Unilever's 2009 policy change under Paul Polman explicitly discontinued quarterly profit guidance for the same stated reason; Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal's survey findings are widely cited in corporate governance and accounting research as foundational evidence for the real, measurable cost of the earnings-management behavior this skill targets.
**Impact:** Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal's survey found a majority of CFOs would cut genuinely value-creating discretionary spending specifically to meet a quarterly earnings target, a direct, self-reported measure of value destroyed by this specific pressure; Bushee's firm-level analysis found R&D cuts to avoid missing earnings expectations were significantly more pronounced specifically among firms with higher transient institutional ownership, providing firm-level evidence that the composition of investor pressure, not just management preference, drives the severity of this underinvestment pattern.

## Steps

1. **Identify which specific long-term investments (R&D, infrastructure, maintenance, talent development) are being evaluated primarily against their effect on the next quarterly reporting period, rather than against their actual long-term value.** Name the specific investment decisions where this trade-off is live, rather than treating "short-termism" as a vague, undiagnosed cultural complaint.

2. **Evaluate each identified investment on its actual long-term value (net present value, strategic option value) independently of its near-term earnings effect, before considering the reporting-period trade-off.** Separating the two evaluations prevents the near-term effect from silently substituting for the actual long-term value assessment.

3. **Where structurally possible, reduce or eliminate the specific external pressure that creates the trade-off — most directly, discontinuing quarterly earnings guidance.** Per Google's and Unilever's documented examples, removing the specific external benchmark that management is pressured to hit removes the proximate cause of the trade-off, rather than asking managers to resist a pressure that continues to exist.

4. **Where guidance cannot be eliminated, separate the reporting of short-term results from the internal capital-allocation decision process for long-term investments.** Insulate the specific committee, budget process, or decision rights for long-term investment from the individuals and incentives most exposed to quarterly performance pressure.

5. **Examine the composition of investor base and incentive structure, per Bushee's finding that transient institutional ownership specifically predicts this underinvestment pattern.** A company with a high proportion of transient, short-term-trading institutional ownership faces measurably higher pressure toward this specific trade-off than one with a more long-term-oriented investor base — this is a diagnosable, not merely cultural, risk factor.

6. **Make the trade-off explicit and visible when it does occur, rather than allowing it to happen silently within routine budget cuts.** If a genuinely positive-long-term-value investment is cut specifically to protect near-term earnings, document that this specific trade-off was made and why — visibility creates accountability and a record for later evaluation, rather than letting the practice become an invisible, routine default.

## Rules

- Treat quarterly-earnings-driven cuts to genuinely positive-long-term-value investment as a specific, diagnosable, measurable pattern — not a vague cultural complaint — and identify the specific decisions where it is currently live.
- Evaluate long-term investments on their actual long-term value independently of their near-term reporting effect, before considering any trade-off between the two.
- Where feasible, remove the specific external pressure (quarterly guidance) that creates the proximate incentive for the trade-off, rather than relying on individual managers to resist it.
- Make any genuine trade-off between near-term earnings and long-term value explicit and documented when it occurs, rather than letting it happen as an invisible default within routine budget decisions.

## Examples

**Ending quarterly guidance:** A publicly traded company facing recurring pressure to cut its multi-year R&D program to hit analyst-expected quarterly earnings numbers discontinues providing quarterly guidance, following the pattern of Google's and Unilever's documented policy changes. Analyst and investor reactions are initially mixed, but the company's subsequent capital-allocation decisions on long-term R&D projects are no longer directly driven by the need to hit a recurring short-term external benchmark.

**Insulating the capital-allocation process:** A company facing recurring earnings pressure separates its long-term infrastructure investment decisions into a dedicated capital committee evaluated on multi-year value creation, distinct from the operating units whose compensation and internal evaluation are tied to quarterly performance — reducing the degree to which the same individuals face conflicting pressure across both decisions.

**Diagnosing investor-base risk:** A management team, aware of Bushee's finding linking transient institutional ownership to R&D cuts, examines its own investor base and finds a high proportion of short-term-trading institutional holders. Recognizing this as a measurable risk factor for future earnings-driven underinvestment, the team explicitly documents and monitors R&D spending against reporting-period pressure going forward, rather than assuming the risk doesn't apply to their specific situation.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating short-termism as an unavoidable cultural fact of public markets rather than a specific, diagnosable, and in some cases structurally correctable pattern.** Google's and Unilever's documented policy changes demonstrate the pressure can be structurally reduced, not merely complained about.
- **Cutting a genuinely positive-long-term-value investment to hit a quarterly number without evaluating and documenting the actual long-term value being sacrificed.** This lets the trade-off happen silently and prevents later accountability or learning from the decision.
- **Assuming all investor pressure toward short-term earnings is equivalent, without examining the actual composition of the investor base.** Per Bushee's findings, transient institutional ownership specifically predicts this pattern more than ownership overall — the risk is measurable and diagnosable, not uniform.
- **Relying on individual managers' willpower to resist earnings pressure rather than structurally reducing the external pressure itself.** Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal's survey found this trade-off reflects rational responses to real incentives, not a failure of individual discipline that better managers could simply overcome.

## When NOT to Use

- For genuinely low-value or speculative long-term spending being reconsidered on its actual merits — this skill defends investments with genuine, independently-verified long-term value against short-term reporting pressure specifically; it is not a justification for continuing any long-term spending regardless of its actual value.
- For private companies with no public quarterly reporting cycle and no analyst guidance pressure — the specific mechanism this skill addresses depends on the existence of a recurring short-term external benchmark; it doesn't apply in its absence.
- When a company's near-term earnings pressure reflects a genuine, acute solvency or liquidity concern rather than routine reporting-cycle pressure — in a genuine near-term survival situation, near-term cash preservation can legitimately take priority over long-term value, which is a different situation from the routine earnings-management trade-off this skill targets.
