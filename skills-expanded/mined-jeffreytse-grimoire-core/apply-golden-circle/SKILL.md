# Apply Golden Circle

Restructure a vision, pitch, or call to action to lead with Why (the belief or purpose driving the work), then How (the specific approach that makes it distinct), then What (the product or action itself) — because audiences respond to and remember purpose-led communication more than feature-led communication, even when the underlying facts communicated are identical.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** Most communication about a product, initiative, or organization defaults to leading with What (the specific thing being offered) and, if there's room, What it does differently (How), with the underlying Why left implicit or omitted entirely. Sinek's specific claim is that this ordering is backwards relative to what actually moves an audience to action or loyalty: leading explicitly with the belief or purpose behind the work, before describing the approach or the specific offering, produces stronger persuasion and identification than leading with the offering itself — the same facts, communicated in reverse order, produce a measurably different audience response.

**Sinek, "Start with Why" (2009) and associated TED talk:** Sinek's framework — the "Golden Circle" of three concentric layers, Why (purpose or belief) at the center, How (the specific approach) in the middle, and What (the product or action) on the outside — argues that most communicators work from the outside in (What, then How, then Why, if at all), while the organizations and leaders Sinek documents as unusually effective at inspiring loyalty and action communicate from the inside out, leading explicitly with Why. Sinek's TED talk presenting this framework is among the most-viewed TED talks ever recorded, and the book has been widely adopted in leadership and marketing communication training specifically for its practical reordering principle: state the purpose first, then the differentiated approach, then the specific offering — rather than the reverse.

**Adopted by:** "Start with Why" and the Golden Circle framework are widely taught in marketing, leadership communication, and brand strategy training; Sinek's TED talk on the framework is among the most-viewed talks in TED's history, reflecting broad practitioner and general-audience adoption of the reordering principle it presents.
**Impact:** The framework's practical, widely-adopted claim is specifically about message ordering: communicating the same underlying facts (a product's features, an organization's approach) in Why-How-What order rather than What-How-Why order produces measurably stronger audience identification and motivation to act — a claim Sinek supports with case comparisons between organizations that lead with purpose and otherwise-comparable organizations that lead with product specifications alone.

## Steps

1. **Identify the actual Why before drafting any communication — the specific belief or purpose driving the work, stated as a conviction, not as a mission-statement platitude.** A genuine Why is specific enough that a reasonable person could disagree with it; a vague Why ("we care about quality") isn't specific enough to differentiate the message.

2. **Identify the How — the specific approach or method that embodies that Why and distinguishes it from alternatives.** This is the concrete mechanism by which the stated belief gets put into practice, not a restatement of the belief itself.

3. **Identify the What last — the specific product, service, or action being offered, understood as the tangible proof of the Why and How rather than as the primary message.** The What should read as evidence supporting the Why and How, not as the headline.

4. **Draft or restructure the actual communication in Why-How-What order, deliberately reversing the more common What-How-Why default.** State the belief first, the differentiated approach second, and the specific offering or ask last.

5. **Test the restructured communication against the audience's actual response, not just against whether it "sounds inspiring."** The framework's value is in producing a measurably different response (identification, motivation, recall), not merely satisfying rhetorical style — check whether the reordering actually changes how the audience receives and acts on the message.

6. **Apply the same ordering consistently across channels — a pitch, a product page, an internal announcement — rather than treating Why-first framing as a one-time exercise for a single flagship communication.** The framework's claimed benefit compounds with consistent use across an organization's communication, not from a single instance.

## Rules

- Lead with a specific, genuine Why — a stated belief specific enough that someone could reasonably disagree with it — not a generic value statement that could apply to any organization.
- Present the How as the concrete mechanism that embodies the Why, not as a restatement of the Why in different words.
- Present the What last, as evidence supporting the Why and How, rather than as the message's headline.
- Apply the ordering consistently across the range of communications an audience encounters, not only in a single flagship piece.

## Examples

**Product pitch restructuring:** A team's product pitch originally opens with a feature list (What), then explains how the features work (How), with the underlying purpose left implicit. Restructured in Why-How-What order — opening with the specific problem the team believes is worth solving and why it matters, then the distinct approach taken, then the specific product features as evidence — the same facts produce a noticeably stronger audience response in follow-up conversations and questions.

**Internal announcement:** A company announcing an organizational change leads with the specific belief driving the decision (the Why) and the particular approach chosen to act on that belief (the How), before detailing the specific structural changes (the What) — producing measurably less resistance and more constructive questions than an earlier internal announcement that opened directly with the structural changes themselves.

**Brand communication consistency:** An organization that successfully led a flagship keynote with Why-first framing continues to apply the same ordering to its product pages, hiring materials, and internal communications, rather than treating the reordering as a one-time speech-writing technique — reflecting the framework's claim that the benefit compounds with consistent application.

## Common Mistakes

- **Stating a generic, non-specific Why** ("we care about our customers") that could apply to virtually any organization, rather than a genuine, specific belief that differentiates the communication.
- **Restating the Why as the How**, rather than describing a concrete, distinct mechanism that embodies the stated belief.
- **Leading with the What out of habit**, especially under time pressure, reverting to feature-first communication even after having identified a genuine Why and How.
- **Applying Why-first framing to a single flagship communication while leaving routine, everyday communications in the default What-first order**, missing the framework's claimed compounding benefit from consistent application.

## When NOT to Use

- For purely transactional, low-stakes communication where audience identification and motivation aren't the goal — a routine status update or a simple factual notice doesn't need Why-How-What restructuring.
- When there genuinely is no distinct, specific Why behind the work being communicated — forcing a fabricated purpose statement onto a purely commodity offering produces hollow messaging rather than genuine differentiation.
- When the audience specifically needs the What first for practical reasons (a reference document, technical specification, or safety instruction where immediate, direct information is the actual requirement) — Why-How-What ordering is for persuasive and motivational communication specifically, not for every communication format.
