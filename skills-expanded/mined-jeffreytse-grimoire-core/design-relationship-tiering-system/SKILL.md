# Design Relationship Tiering System

Assign each contact in your professional network an explicit tier based on their value and depth of relationship, set a minimum contact frequency for each tier, and actively schedule touchpoints against that cadence — because an unmanaged network defaults to contacting only whoever is currently top-of-mind, silently losing valuable but infrequently-encountered relationships to simple neglect.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** Human relationship-maintenance capacity is finite and does not scale with the number of people you meet — without an explicit system, contact frequency defaults to recency and convenience (whoever you happen to run into or think of), not to a contact's actual value or the relationship's fragility. A contact who is high-value but low-frequency-by-default (someone you only naturally encounter once every few years) is exactly the relationship an unmanaged network loses first, because nothing in an ad hoc system prompts a touchpoint before the relationship has already gone cold. Explicit tiering with a minimum cadence per tier converts relationship maintenance from a passive, memory-dependent process into an active, scheduled one — matching maintenance effort to actual relationship value rather than to accidental recency.

**Robin Dunbar (1992) and subsequent research:** Dunbar's foundational research, and the substantial body of subsequent work it generated, established that humans maintain cognitively meaningful relationships in a limited, layered structure — a small inner circle of very close relationships, surrounded by successively larger but shallower layers of less-frequently-maintained relationships, with total capacity for meaningfully maintained relationships bounded regardless of how many people are nominally "in the network." This provides the underlying constraint that makes explicit tiering necessary: since maintenance capacity for close, effortful contact is genuinely limited, an explicit system for allocating that limited capacity across a growing network is required once the network exceeds what memory alone can track.

**McDonald & Rogers, "Key Account Management" (1998):** The foundational, industry-standard framework for business-to-business account management, formalizing tiered account segmentation (commonly by account value and strategic importance) paired with an explicitly differentiated contact-frequency requirement per tier — top-tier strategic accounts receive scheduled, frequent, senior-level contact, while lower-tier accounts receive lighter, less frequent touch. This tiered-cadence structure, adopted broadly across B2B sales organizations, is the direct organizational analog to the personal-networking version of the same technique: allocate scarce relationship-maintenance capacity according to an explicit tier, not according to whoever happens to reach out first.

**Keith Ferrazzi, "Never Eat Alone" (2005, 1M+ copies):** Ferrazzi's documented personal networking practice includes a systematic tracking system for his contacts, explicitly built to prevent valuable relationships from being lost to the natural drift of time and distance — recording specific personal details and deliberately scheduling recurring contact with people he has identified as important to maintain a relationship with, rather than relying on chance encounters or memory to sustain those connections.

**Adopted by:** Key account management's tiered-cadence structure is standard practice across B2B sales and enterprise account management organizations; "personal CRM" tools and practices (a now-established productivity category, reflecting exactly this technique applied to individual professional networking) have grown specifically to formalize Ferrazzi-style systematic relationship tracking and scheduled cadence for individuals managing large professional networks.
**Impact:** Key account management's tiered contact-cadence structure is credited in the sales-management literature with materially higher retention and expansion rates for top-tier accounts specifically because those accounts receive scheduled, proactive contact rather than only reactive contact when a customer initiates it; Ferrazzi's documented systematic-tracking approach to personal networking is presented as directly enabling the breadth and depth of his professional relationship network relative to networking practiced through unaided memory alone.

## Steps

1. **Define a small number of explicit tiers before assigning anyone to one.** Three to five tiers (e.g., S/A/B/C, or a similarly labeled scheme) is typically enough — too many tiers add administrative overhead without improving the actual prioritization decision.

2. **Define the criteria for each tier explicitly, based on relationship value and depth, not on how recently or easily you happen to have been in contact.** Value criteria might include the depth of trust, the strategic or professional importance of the relationship, or a track record of mutual help — deliberately exclude "how easy this person currently is to reach" from the tier-assignment criteria, since that is exactly the recency bias the system exists to correct.

3. **Assign a minimum contact frequency to each tier.** For example: top tier — at least once a year, with deliberate effort even if it requires travel or scheduling difficulty; middle tiers — a longer interval, still explicitly scheduled; lowest tier — no active maintenance obligation, contact only if it happens naturally.

4. **Record the tier and last-contact date in a system you actually check on a recurring schedule** — a spreadsheet, a note-taking app, or a dedicated personal-CRM tool, whichever you'll reliably revisit rather than one that's technically comprehensive but never opened.

5. **Schedule a recurring review (monthly or quarterly) to check which top-tier contacts are approaching or past their minimum cadence, and proactively reach out before the relationship has gone cold.** The system's value comes specifically from the review being proactive and scheduled, not from the tier list existing passively.

6. **Re-tier contacts periodically as relationships genuinely change.** A contact's tier should reflect the current, real state of the relationship's value and depth — update tiers deliberately when a relationship's importance changes, rather than leaving initial tier assignments static indefinitely.

7. **Combine this system with genuine, substantive engagement at each touchpoint, not a purely transactional check-in.** The scheduled cadence ensures the contact happens; the quality of what happens during that contact (see `apply-non-transactional-giving`, `apply-visible-note-taking-elicitation`) determines whether the relationship actually deepens rather than merely persisting.

## Rules

- Define tier-assignment criteria around relationship value and depth, explicitly excluding current ease-of-contact — recency and convenience are the biases this system exists to correct, not inputs to the tiering decision.
- Set a genuinely differentiated minimum cadence per tier; a system where every tier gets the same contact frequency provides no additional prioritization value over having no tiers at all.
- Schedule a recurring, calendared review specifically to check upcoming or lapsed cadence obligations — an unreviewed tier list decays into exactly the passive, memory-dependent system this technique replaces.
- Re-tier contacts as relationships genuinely change, rather than treating initial tier assignments as permanent.

## Examples

**Professional network maintenance:** A senior professional with hundreds of professional contacts tiers a specific subset (mentors, former close collaborators, key long-term contacts) into a top tier requiring at least annual contact, a middle tier requiring contact every two to three years, and leaves the remaining contacts untiered with no active maintenance obligation. A quarterly calendar reminder surfaces which top-tier contacts are approaching their annual deadline, prompting proactive outreach before the relationship goes cold from simple neglect.

**Enterprise sales analog:** An account management team segments its customer base into three tiers by strategic account value, assigning quarterly executive check-ins to top-tier accounts, semi-annual check-ins to mid-tier accounts, and reactive-only support to the remaining accounts. Top-tier account retention and expansion rates are measurably higher than they were before the tiered-cadence system was formalized, attributed specifically to proactive rather than purely reactive account contact.

**Combining with note-taking elicitation:** A person who regularly meets promising junior professionals for a single conversation, per `apply-visible-note-taking-elicitation`, tags the most impressive of those contacts into a top tier immediately after the conversation and schedules a follow-up touchpoint within the next year, converting a one-time encounter into an actively maintained long-term relationship rather than letting it lapse by default.

## Common Mistakes

- **Tiering contacts by how easy or recent contact currently is, rather than by actual relationship value.** This simply formalizes the recency bias the system is meant to correct, rather than fixing it.
- **Creating too many tiers, adding administrative overhead without improving the actual prioritization decision.** Three to five clearly distinguished tiers is typically sufficient; finer-grained tiering rarely earns its added maintenance cost.
- **Building a detailed tiering system but never scheduling a recurring review of it.** An unreviewed tier list is inert — the actual value comes from the scheduled, proactive check against upcoming cadence obligations, not from the existence of the tier assignments alone.
- **Treating the scheduled touchpoint itself as sufficient, regardless of its substance.** A purely transactional, low-effort check-in that satisfies the cadence requirement without any genuine engagement fails to actually deepen the relationship — pair the cadence with substantive engagement at each touchpoint.

## When NOT to Use

- For a network small enough to maintain reliably through memory and ad hoc contact alone — the overhead of building and maintaining an explicit tiering system isn't justified until the network has grown past what unaided memory can track.
- As a substitute for genuine relationship investment — a rigorously maintained cadence with hollow, transactional check-ins produces persistence without depth; the system schedules opportunities for genuine engagement, it does not replace the need for it.
- In close personal relationships (family, close friends) where explicit scheduling would itself feel transactional and damage the relationship — this technique is intended for the broader professional network beyond the small inner circle already maintained through ordinary personal closeness.
