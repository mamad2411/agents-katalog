# Apply Graduated Entry Point Technique

When an audience isn't motivated by the actual goal but has a specific want of their own, use that existing want, unmodified, as the literal entry point to get them engaged — then execute a planned, disclosed transition to the real objective once engagement is secured.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** Trying to motivate an audience directly with a goal they aren't ready for or don't yet value produces the same result as warning children unmoved by an abstract danger they don't understand — no movement. Using their own already-existing, concrete want as the vehicle gets them moving on their own terms; the transition to the actual objective happens only once they're already engaged, when it's far easier to introduce.

**Adopted by:** Madeline Hunter's "anticipatory set" model — opening a lesson with something students already want or care about before pivoting into the actual curricular objective — became a standard component of U.S. teacher-training curricula (ITIP) after her 1982 "Mastery Teaching." Freedman & Fraser's foot-in-the-door research (1966) documented compliance-escalation from an initial small, wanted request; modern inbound-marketing methodology (documented in practices like HubSpot's) runs the same structure at scale: offer free content or a tool the audience already wants (a "lead magnet"), then transition to the actual product offer once attention is earned.

**Impact:** Hunter's anticipatory-set model is credited with measurably improving lesson engagement in teacher-effectiveness research by giving students a reason to attend to a lesson before its actual content is revealed, rather than requiring them to already value the curricular objective. Foot-in-the-door research shows compliance rates rise substantially once a person has already said yes to a related, wanted initial request — the same underlying mechanism of securing engagement on the audience's terms before the actual ask.

**Why best (continued):** This differs from the closest neighboring techniques already in this repo. `apply-motivational-interviewing` evokes a client's own motivation *about the same target* — it never substitutes an unrelated want as a bridge; this skill specifically permits the entry-point vehicle to be different from the final objective, provided the eventual transition is planned and disclosed. `design-differentiated-instruction-plan` explicitly states that differentiation "adjusts the pathway, not the destination" and treats interest as one transparent input to lesson design, not a vehicle later swapped for a different goal. This skill's distinguishing mechanism is exactly that graduated substitution — entry on the audience's want, transition to the practitioner's actual objective — which neither of those skills performs.

Sources: Hunter, *Mastery Teaching* (1982); Freedman & Fraser, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (1966); Miller & Rollnick, *Motivational Interviewing* (2013), cited for contrast.

## Steps

1. **Identify the actual objective you need the audience to reach.** State it precisely — this is the destination the entry point exists to lead toward, not the thing you'll present first.

2. **Diagnose what the specific audience already, concretely wants.** This must be a real, specific want of theirs — not a generic assumption about what "people like this" want, and not the deeper objective restated in different words.

3. **Use that existing want, unmodified, as the literal entry point.** Deliver genuinely on the entry-point want — don't offer it as bait and then fail to provide it. The engagement has to be real, or the technique collapses into a false promise that undermines the transition that follows.

4. **Plan the transition to the actual objective before starting, not improvised after engagement.** Decide in advance what the bridge from the entry-point vehicle to the real objective will look like, and how it will be introduced once the audience is engaged — an unplanned, ad hoc transition is where this technique most often fails.

5. **Disclose the transition once engagement is secured — don't conceal it indefinitely.** The audience should come to understand, at the point of transition, that the entry point was a deliberate step toward something further, not have the real objective sprung on them as an unrelated surprise they never agreed to.

6. **Verify the entry-point vehicle bears a legitimate, honest connection to the real objective.** The transition works when the audience can see, once it's revealed, why that particular entry point led somewhere connected and worthwhile — an entry point with no real bridge to the objective reads as a bait-and-switch once revealed.

## Rules

- Always deliver genuinely on the entry-point want — using it as bait without actually providing it destroys trust and undermines the transition.
- Plan the transition to the real objective in advance — don't improvise it after engagement is already secured.
- Disclose the transition once engagement is secured — never conceal indefinitely that the entry point was a deliberate step toward a further goal.
- The entry-point vehicle must have a legitimate, explainable connection to the real objective — an arbitrary or unrelated vehicle reads as deceptive once revealed, even if the individual steps were each honest.

## Examples

**Trigger:** A teacher needs students to engage with a curricular objective they have no intrinsic interest in.
→ Open the lesson with something students already care about — a game, a story, a question tied to their own interests — genuinely engaging on those terms first. Once attention is secured, make a planned, disclosed transition into the actual curricular content, explicitly connecting the opening hook to the lesson's real objective rather than abandoning it once attention is captured.

**Trigger:** A product team wants to convert visitors who have no current interest in the paid product, but do want a specific free resource the company can genuinely provide.
→ Offer the free resource the visitor actually wants, and deliver it in full. Once engagement is established, transition — disclosed, not hidden — into presenting the paid offering, explaining the legitimate connection between the free resource and the paid product rather than treating the free offer as a bait-and-switch pretext.

## Common Mistakes

- **Offering the entry-point want as bait without actually delivering it.** This destroys the trust the subsequent transition depends on and turns the technique into a deceptive pretext rather than a genuine graduated entry point.
- **Improvising the transition after engagement instead of planning it in advance.** An unplanned pivot to the real objective tends to feel abrupt or manipulative to the audience, undermining the technique's own mechanism.
- **Never disclosing the transition, letting the audience believe the entry point was the whole interaction.** Concealing the deliberate structure indefinitely crosses from a legitimate teaching/influence technique into manipulation.
- **Choosing an entry-point vehicle with no honest, explainable connection to the real objective.** If the bridge can't be explained once revealed, the technique reads as bait-and-switch rather than a genuine graduated path.

## When NOT to Use

- When the audience's existing motivation can be evoked directly about the actual goal itself — use `apply-motivational-interviewing` instead; that technique stays on the real target throughout and doesn't need a substitute vehicle.
- When the entry-point want has no legitimate, honest bridge to the real objective — forcing an unrelated vehicle onto an unrelated goal degrades this technique into deception regardless of how it's executed.
- When transparency from the very first interaction is required by context (regulated disclosures, informed consent, fiduciary relationships) — those contexts require stating the real objective upfront, not graduating into it.
