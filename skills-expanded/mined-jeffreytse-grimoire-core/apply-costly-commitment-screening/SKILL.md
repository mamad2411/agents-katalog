# Apply Costly Commitment Screening

When many people request your scarce time or mentorship and a conversation cannot reliably reveal their genuine commitment level, require a specific, costly, objectively verifiable unit of self-directed output before engaging — because completing that output is a filter that only genuinely committed people pass, while a conversation alone can be passed by anyone regardless of actual follow-through.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** This is the mirror image of costly signaling: instead of the scarce-resource holder sending a costly signal to attract others (as in `apply-talent-value-signaling`), the scarce-resource holder here designs a costly test and lets others sort themselves by whether they complete it — a mechanism formally known in economics as screening. The specific design requirement is that the test cost almost nothing to the genuinely committed (who were going to produce that output anyway on the path to their actual goal) while being prohibitively effortful for someone who wants access or validation without genuine follow-through — this asymmetry in the cost of completing the test is what makes it an effective filter, not the specific content of the test itself.

**Spence, "Job Market Signaling" (1973):** Spence's foundational work on informational asymmetry established that when one party cannot directly verify another party's underlying quality (motivation, ability, genuine commitment), a costly action that is cheap for the genuine type and expensive for the non-genuine type can substitute for direct verification — because only someone who actually possesses the underlying quality would find the action worth the cost. Requiring a completed body of self-directed work before offering scarce mentorship applies exactly this logic in the reverse direction from a company signaling to attract talent: here, the party requesting access is the one whose type needs to be revealed.

**Rothschild & Stiglitz, "Equilibrium in Competitive Insurance Markets" (1976):** This companion body of economic theory to Spence's signaling work formalized "screening" — the specific mechanism where the party who lacks information (here, the mentor, who cannot directly observe a requester's true commitment level) designs a menu or test that different underlying types will respond to differently, allowing the uninformed party to sort applicants by type without needing to assess commitment directly through conversation or claims. A completed, verifiable body of work (a specific count of finished attempts) is precisely this kind of screening device: it requires no judgment call from the mentor and produces a hard, binary sorting outcome.

**Adopted by:** The underlying screening-and-signaling framework from Spence and Rothschild & Stiglitz is foundational, Nobel-recognized economic theory (Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in part for this work) taught across economics and information-design curricula; the specific practice of requiring a demonstrated body of self-directed output before offering scarce mentorship time is widely used informally by experienced practitioners across creative and technical fields as a low-cost way to filter high volumes of access requests.
**Impact:** Spence's signaling framework and Rothschild & Stiglitz's screening framework together provide the formal, peer-reviewed, Nobel-recognized theoretical basis for why a costly, verifiable pre-commitment requirement reliably separates genuinely committed applicants from those seeking access without follow-through, without requiring the scarce-resource holder to make a direct, unreliable judgment call about someone's motivation from conversation alone.

## Steps

1. **Identify a specific, costly, objectively verifiable unit of output that is directly on the path to the goal the requester claims to want.** The requirement should be something a genuinely committed person would produce anyway in pursuit of their stated goal — not an arbitrary hoop unrelated to their actual objective.

2. **Set a specific, unambiguous threshold, not a vague one.** "A meaningful body of work" is not verifiable and invites negotiation; a specific count ("thirty completed attempts") is binary and requires no subjective judgment call from you to assess whether it's been met.

3. **State the requirement plainly and offer the follow-up engagement as a genuine, specific commitment once it's met.** The screening only works if completing the requirement genuinely unlocks the promised access — a bait-and-switch after the requirement is met defeats the mechanism and damages trust in it for future use.

4. **Do not evaluate or coach the requester before the threshold is met.** The filter's value comes from requiring self-directed completion without your involvement; providing feedback or partial engagement before the threshold undermines the screening property by making the pre-commitment easier to satisfy without genuine independent follow-through.

5. **Expect the large majority of requesters not to return.** Since the screening test is specifically designed to be cheap for the genuinely committed and costly for everyone else, a high non-completion rate is the expected, correct outcome of the mechanism working — not evidence the bar is set too high.

6. **When someone does return having met the threshold, treat their having done so as strong evidence of genuine commitment, and invest accordingly.** The completed body of work is itself informative beyond simply having crossed the threshold — review it as part of the subsequent engagement, since it reveals real information about their current level and approach that a first conversation would not have.

## Rules

- Choose a screening requirement that is directly on the path to the requester's stated goal — an unrelated or arbitrary hoop does not carry the same signaling information and reads as gatekeeping for its own sake.
- Set a specific, binary, verifiable threshold rather than a vague or subjectively-judged one — the mechanism depends on requiring no discretionary evaluation to determine whether it's been met.
- Genuinely honor the commitment once the threshold is met — the screening mechanism only continues to work if completing it reliably unlocks the promised engagement.
- Do not provide meaningful feedback or engagement before the threshold is met — early involvement undermines the self-directed-completion property the filter depends on.

## Examples

**Mentorship gatekeeping:** An experienced short-video creator is repeatedly asked for consultations by people wanting to learn the craft. Rather than accepting requests based on how motivated someone sounds in a message, the creator requires thirty completed videos before offering a free consultation — the overwhelming majority of requesters never return, and those who do arrive with a concrete body of work that makes the eventual consultation substantially more useful than an equivalent conversation would have been at the outset.

**Technical mentorship:** A senior engineer frequently fields requests to mentor junior developers interested in a specific specialization. Instead of assessing interest through conversation, the engineer asks each requester to complete and submit a small, specific open-source contribution in that specialization first — those who do so demonstrate both genuine follow-through and a concrete starting skill level the engineer can build on.

**Investment analog:** An angel investor who receives many pitch requests from aspiring founders asks each to first build and demonstrate a minimum working prototype before a meeting is scheduled — filtering for founders who will do the necessary work without direct funding or validation already in hand, which the investor has found correlates with the sustained execution the venture will eventually require.

## Common Mistakes

- **Setting a vague, subjectively-judged threshold instead of a specific, verifiable one.** A vague bar invites negotiation and requires ongoing judgment calls that reintroduce the exact assessment problem the mechanism was meant to avoid.
- **Requiring an arbitrary task unrelated to the requester's actual goal.** This produces resentment without carrying real signaling information, since completing an unrelated hoop doesn't reveal anything about commitment to the actual pursuit.
- **Providing partial engagement or feedback before the threshold is met.** This defeats the self-directed-completion property that makes the screen informative, and can turn the requirement into an unpaid extraction of your time in installments.
- **Treating a low return rate as evidence the requirement should be lowered.** A low completion rate is the expected, correct signature of a working screen — it reflects that most requesters lacked the commitment the filter is designed to detect, not that the bar is miscalibrated.

## When NOT to Use

- When the volume of requests is low enough that direct conversation and judgment can reliably assess genuine commitment without a formal screen — the overhead of designing and enforcing a costly test isn't justified at low request volume.
- When no meaningful, verifiable, on-path output exists for the domain in question — the mechanism depends on a genuine, checkable unit of self-directed work; forcing an artificial substitute produces the arbitrary-hoop failure mode instead of real signaling information.
- In contexts with a formal, existing evaluation process already suited to assessing commitment and quality (a structured application or interview process) — a costly pre-commitment screen is a substitute for that assessment specifically when no such process exists, not an addition on top of one that already works.
