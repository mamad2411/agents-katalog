# Apply Conquest-to-Governance Shift

At the moment a venture or leader has just won decisively, deliberately diagnose that the offense-phase playbook which produced the win is not the playbook the consolidation phase requires — and switch operating models at the transition point, rather than continuing the approach that worked for winning into the very different task of governing what was won.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Origin:** Jia Yi's 过秦论 ("On the Faults of Qin"), written early in the Han dynasty, analyzes why the Qin state — which had just achieved something no state before it had managed, unifying all of China through decades of relentless military conquest and harsh centralized legalist control — collapsed within about fifteen years of that unification, brought down in significant part by rebellions its own former subjects. Jia Yi's diagnosis was structural: the aggressive, centralizing, harsh methods that had been exactly right for conquering rival states were the wrong methods for governing a newly unified population that needed consolidation and legitimacy, not continued conquest-era severity. The line "攻守之势异也" — the circumstances of offense and defense are different — states the core finding directly: a strategy correct for one phase can be actively destructive when carried unchanged into the next.

**Adopted by:** Marshall Goldsmith's *What Got You Here Won't Get You There* (2007), based on extensive executive-coaching work, documents the same structural pattern in a modern setting: the specific behaviors that drive a person's or organization's success at one level or phase are frequently the exact behaviors that undermine success at the next level or phase, precisely because they were never re-examined once they'd proven effective. The same pattern is a documented, cross-company theme in post-merger-integration research (deals that succeed at closing but fail at integration specifically because deal-mode aggressiveness and control persist into a phase requiring trust-building and cultural integration instead) and in the well-documented, repeated pattern of founder-led companies encountering governance crises after scaling or going public, when the founder's high-autonomy, low-process conquest-phase style is retained past the point where the organization needs institutionalized governance.

**Impact:** Jia Yi's own account attributes Qin's rapid collapse specifically to this mismatch, not to any weakening of Qin's capability — the same methods that unified the empire remained available, but were the wrong tool for the new phase. Modern instances of the same failure (retained deal-mode aggression damaging post-acquisition integration, founder-mode governance crises after scaling) are documented as a recurring, cross-organizational pattern rather than a single company's misfortune, giving this a falsifiable, repeatedly-observed structure rather than a one-off anecdote.

**Why best:** This differs from the closest adjacent skills already in this repo. `apply-strategic-momentum` covers building potential and striking at peak advantage — it stops at the moment of the win itself and doesn't address what operating model the post-win phase requires. `apply-tactical-adaptation` explicitly holds strategy constant and adapts only tactics — the opposite of what this situation calls for, where the strategy itself, not just its tactical execution, needs to change. `apply-post-crisis-trap` addresses an individual crisis contributor's own personal risk once a crisis resolves, a different actor and a different remedy than an organization or leader recognizing that its own winning playbook must be replaced. This skill's distinguishing claim is that the switch is a deliberate strategic decision to be made at the transition point, not a tactical adjustment or an individual's self-preservation move.

## Steps

### 1. Recognize the decisive win as a phase boundary, not just an outcome

Treat the moment of decisive victory — the closed acquisition, the achieved market dominance, the ended crisis — as the specific point at which the operating requirements change, not merely as the successful conclusion of the previous phase. The win itself is the signal to ask what's needed next, not a signal that the same approach should continue.

### 2. Name specifically what the offense-phase playbook consisted of

State explicitly what made the winning approach effective — aggressive resource concentration, high individual autonomy, tolerance for chaos and ambiguity, rapid unilateral decision-making, harsh enforcement of a narrow set of priorities — so there's something concrete to evaluate against what the new phase actually requires, rather than a vague sense that "things need to change."

### 3. Diagnose what the consolidation/governance phase actually requires

Identify the specific requirements of the new phase — broader legitimacy with a wider population/stakeholder base, institutionalized process rather than ad hoc individual judgment, trust-building rather than continued pressure, patience with stability rather than continued disruption — and check each element of the offense-phase playbook against whether it helps or actively harms these new requirements.

### 4. Deliberately retire the elements of the offense playbook that don't transfer

For each offense-phase behavior that doesn't serve the governance phase's requirements, make an explicit decision to retire it rather than letting it persist by default because it worked before. The harm in Jia Yi's account came specifically from continuing methods that had been correct, unmodified, into a context where they were no longer correct.

### 5. Install the governance-phase playbook explicitly, don't assume it will emerge

Build the new phase's requirements deliberately — the institutionalized processes, the broader legitimacy-building, the different leadership behaviors — rather than assuming that stopping the offense-phase behaviors alone is sufficient. The transition requires actively installing what's needed next, not just discontinuing what came before.

### 6. Expect resistance to the switch, including from the offense-phase's own successful actors

The people and structures that were most effective and most rewarded during the offense phase often have the strongest incentive to keep operating the same way, since it's what made them successful — anticipate this resistance rather than assuming everyone will naturally recognize and accept that the phase has changed.

## Rules

- Treat the decisive win as the signal to re-evaluate the operating model, not as validation to continue it — the win being real doesn't mean the method that produced it remains correct going forward.
- Name the offense-phase playbook's specific elements explicitly before deciding what to keep and what to retire — a vague sense that "something needs to change" doesn't produce an actionable transition.
- Actively install the governance-phase requirements rather than assuming they'll emerge once the offense-phase behaviors stop.
- Expect and plan for resistance to the switch, particularly from people whose success and reward were built on the offense-phase approach.

## Examples

**Trigger:** A company completes a major, hard-fought acquisition after months of aggressive, high-pressure deal-making, and the deal team remains in place managing the integration with the same aggressive, controlling approach that closed the deal.
→ Recognize the closed deal as the phase boundary. Name what made the deal team effective (aggressive negotiation, tight information control, rapid unilateral decisions) and check it against what integration actually needs (trust-building across the combined organization, cultural integration, broader buy-in). Deliberately retire the negotiation-mode control and install integration-appropriate collaborative processes instead of assuming the deal-closing team's approach will work equally well for integration.

**Trigger:** A startup's founder leads the company through an existential crisis with decisive, high-autonomy, rapid unilateral action, then continues operating the same way after the company stabilizes and scales.
→ Treat the crisis's resolution as the phase boundary rather than continuing the crisis-mode approach by default. Name the crisis-mode behaviors explicitly (rapid unilateral decisions, minimal process, high personal control) and check them against what a stabilized, scaling organization actually needs (delegated decision-making, institutionalized process, broader trust). Deliberately build the governance-phase structures rather than assuming the approach that saved the company will also successfully run it at scale.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the win as proof the method should continue unchanged.** The win validates that the method worked for the phase it was used in — it says nothing about whether the same method fits the next phase's different requirements.
- **Stopping the offense-phase behaviors without actively installing what the governance phase requires.** Discontinuing the old approach isn't the same as building the new one; the transition needs deliberate construction, not just subtraction.
- **Underestimating resistance from the offense phase's own most successful actors.** The people most rewarded by the winning approach often have the least incentive to recognize that it needs to change.
- **Waiting for an obvious crisis before switching playbooks.** By the time the governance-phase mismatch has produced a visible crisis (Qin's rebellions, a failed integration, a governance scandal), the cost of the transition is far higher than if it had been made deliberately at the win itself.

## When NOT to Use

- When the contest isn't actually over — switching to a governance-phase playbook before the win is secure abandons the offense-phase approach prematurely, before it's finished doing its job.
- When the same operating model genuinely does transfer — not every winning approach requires wholesale replacement; the diagnostic step (checking specific elements against the new phase's actual requirements) may find that some elements of the offense playbook are still appropriate, and only the mismatched elements need to change.
- For a single individual's personal post-crisis risk rather than an organization's or leader's strategic playbook — use `apply-post-crisis-trap` for that narrower, individual-level concern.
