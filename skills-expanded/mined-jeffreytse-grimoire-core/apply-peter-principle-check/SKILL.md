# Apply Peter Principle Check

Before promoting someone based on strong performance in their current role, explicitly test whether the skills that made them successful there are the same skills the next level actually requires — because promotion systems that reward current-role performance alone systematically promote people into roles where those specific skills no longer apply, and this is a measured, documented organizational pattern, not just an old joke about incompetent managers.

## Why This Is Best Practice

**Why best:** The mechanism is specific and mechanical, not a claim about individual incompetence: if promotion decisions are based primarily on demonstrated performance in the current role, and the skills required at the next level are genuinely different (for instance, individual sales performance versus managing a sales team), then the promotion criterion systematically selects for the wrong skill set at the point of promotion. Because promotion typically stops once someone is no longer excelling, organizations following this pattern accumulate people who plateau at the specific level where their old skill set stopped being what the job required — a structural consequence of the selection mechanism itself, not a claim that people generally become incompetent over time.

**Peter & Hull, "The Peter Principle" (1969):** The originating book formalized the observation that, in hierarchies where promotion is based on performance in the current role, employees tend to be promoted based on success at their current level until they reach a role whose requirements no longer match their demonstrated skill set — at which point they stop being promoted, having reached what the book termed their "level of incompetence." The book's core, falsifiable claim is specifically about the selection mechanism (promote based on current performance) producing this predictable pattern, not a claim that people are generally or permanently incompetent.

**Benson, Li & Shue (2019):** This empirical study, examining promotion decisions across a large sample of sales organizations, directly tested the Peter Principle's predictions and found firms disproportionately promoted their best current sales performers into sales management roles, despite those same performance metrics having no positive — and in some specifications a negative — relationship with subsequent success as a manager, and despite better predictors of managerial potential being available in the same data. The study also found that when firms did weigh managerial-potential indicators alongside current-role performance, promoted managers performed measurably better, providing direct causal-adjacent evidence that testing for next-level fit, not just current-role performance, materially improves promotion outcomes.

**Adopted by:** The Peter Principle is widely referenced in organizational-behavior and management literature; Benson, Li & Shue's empirical validation is published in a top-tier economics journal (Quarterly Journal of Economics) and is increasingly cited in HR and people-analytics practice arguing for promotion criteria that test next-level fit rather than relying on current-role performance alone.
**Impact:** Benson, Li & Shue found current-role sales performance had no positive predictive relationship with subsequent managerial performance in their sample, despite being the primary factor firms actually used to make promotion decisions — direct empirical evidence that promotion systems built on current-role performance alone are measurably promoting the wrong people into management roles at scale, and that incorporating a genuine next-level-fit assessment improved outcomes.

## Steps

1. **Before promoting someone, explicitly separate the skills required by their current role from the skills required by the role they'd be promoted into.** Name the specific, concrete skills the new role actually requires — for a move into management, this typically includes delegation, coaching, and cross-team coordination, which are often not the same skills that produced strong individual-contributor performance.

2. **Test for the next-level skills directly, rather than inferring them from current-role performance.** Look for direct evidence of the specific next-level skills — has this person coached or mentored others effectively, delegated successfully, or managed a cross-functional effort — rather than assuming strong current-role output implies these skills are already present.

3. **Where the next-level skill set is genuinely different, create a way to test it before committing to a full promotion.** A trial period with some of the new responsibilities, a temporary lead role on a specific initiative, or a structured mentoring assignment can surface whether the next-level skills are present before an irreversible promotion decision is made.

4. **When someone's current-role performance is excellent but the next-level fit is genuinely uncertain, consider alternative forms of recognition and reward that don't require a role change.** A dual-track career ladder (an individual-contributor track with increasing scope and compensation, parallel to a management track) allows recognizing excellent current-role performance without forcing a promotion into a role that requires a different skill set.

5. **Track promotion outcomes against the specific criteria used to make the promotion decision.** If a pattern emerges where people promoted primarily on current-role performance consistently struggle at the next level, treat this as direct evidence to revise the promotion criteria, following Benson, Li & Shue's finding that incorporating genuine next-level-fit assessment improves outcomes.

6. **If someone is promoted and the next-level fit turns out to be a poor match, treat this as a correctable situation, not a permanent verdict on the person.** A path back to a role matching their actual strengths, without treating it as a demotion or failure, keeps a valuable employee productive rather than stuck in a role mismatched to their skills.

## Rules

- Never treat strong current-role performance alone as sufficient evidence for promotion into a role requiring a genuinely different skill set — test for the next-level skills directly.
- Build a way to observe next-level skills before committing to an irreversible promotion, where the next-level skill set differs meaningfully from the current role's.
- Maintain a non-management, dual-track path for recognizing and rewarding excellent individual-contributor performance without requiring a role change into management.
- Track promotion outcomes against the criteria used to make the decision, and treat a recurring pattern of promoted employees struggling at the next level as direct evidence to revise the promotion process.

## Examples

**Sales-to-management promotion:** A sales organization following Benson, Li & Shue's documented pattern historically promoted its top individual sales performers directly into sales management roles, based primarily on sales performance metrics. After finding that promoted managers' teams underperformed relative to expectations, the organization adds a direct assessment of coaching and delegation ability to the promotion criteria — alongside, not instead of, sales performance — and finds promoted managers subsequently perform better.

**Dual-track career ladder:** A software engineering organization creates a parallel individual-contributor track with senior and staff-level titles, compensation, and scope, alongside its management track — allowing an excellent engineer who has no interest in or aptitude for people management to be recognized and rewarded without being pushed into a management role mismatched to their actual skills.

**Trial before full promotion:** Before promoting a strong individual contributor into a team-lead role, a manager assigns them a temporary lead role on a specific cross-functional initiative first, directly observing their delegation and coordination skills before making the promotion decision permanent.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using current-role performance as the sole or primary criterion for a promotion into a role with a genuinely different skill requirement.** This is the exact mechanism Peter and Hull identified and Benson, Li & Shue empirically validated as producing systematically poor promotion outcomes.
- **Having no non-management path to recognize and reward excellent individual-contributor performance**, forcing high performers into management as the only available form of advancement regardless of fit.
- **Treating a promotion as a one-way, irreversible decision with no path back if the next-level fit turns out to be poor**, which discourages honest evaluation of whether a promotion is actually working out.
- **Failing to track promotion outcomes against the criteria used to make the decision**, missing the direct evidence needed to identify and correct a systematically flawed promotion process.

## When NOT to Use

- When the current role and the next-level role genuinely require the same core skill set — in that case, current-role performance is a reasonably direct predictor of next-level success, and the additional testing this technique requires adds less value.
- In organizations too small to support a dual-track career ladder or trial-promotion structures — the specific structural remedies this technique recommends may not be practical at very small scale, though the underlying diagnostic principle (test for next-level fit specifically) still applies.
- As a reason to withhold promotion indefinitely from someone with genuine next-level potential simply because it hasn't yet been directly demonstrated — the goal is testing for fit before an irreversible commitment, not creating an impossibly high bar that blocks legitimate advancement.
