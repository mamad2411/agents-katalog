# Client Pushback

Most client conflicts in architecture come from an expectation nobody wrote down: what the fee covers, what the budget buys, how long decisions take. This skill gets the architect into the conversation with a clear position, options the client can choose between, and a written record afterwards.

## When to use

- The client keeps adding scope without adding fee.
- The budget or programme can't deliver the brief, and someone has to say so.
- The client wants a design change the architect believes is a mistake.
- Late changes are about to cost time and money.
- Decisions or payments are late and the project is stalling.

## Input

- **The situation:** what happened, in the architect's words.
- **The baseline:** the appointment or fee proposal scope, the agreed brief, the budget and programme, and previous written decisions.
- **History:** relevant emails or meeting notes.
- **What the architect wants** as the outcome, and what they could accept.
- **The relationship:** how important the client is, and how they prefer to communicate.

## Process

1. **Establish the facts and the baseline.** What was agreed, in writing, and where? What has changed? If the baseline was never written down, that is the first problem to fix, and the conversation is partly about fixing it.
2. **Test the architect's own position.** Is the client partly right? Did the practice contribute to the problem? A position that concedes what is fair is stronger.
3. **Name the real issue in one sentence.** For example: "The current brief costs about X more than the budget." Or: "These changes are outside the agreed scope."
4. **Build two to four options** the client can choose from, each with its consequence for cost, time, quality and risk. For example: proceed and pay an additional fee; reduce scope to stay within fee; pause while the client decides. Give the architect's recommendation and the reason.
5. **Write the script.** An opening that states the issue plainly and without blame, the options, the recommendation, and the ask (a decision by a date). Add two or three likely objections with calm answers.
6. **Draft the follow-up email** that records what was discussed, the decision (or the decision still needed and by when), and any fee or programme consequence. Send it within a day.
7. **Flag contractual exposure.** If the issue touches the appointment terms, liability, payment law, or dispute procedures, say that it needs the practice's contract adviser.

## Domain knowledge

- **Lead with the consequence, not the complaint.** "If we add the basement, the planning drawings move by four weeks and the fee increases by X" lands better than "you keep changing things".
- **Give choices, not ultimatums.** Clients accept a cost when they have chosen it.
- **Put it in writing, every time.** A decision that exists only in a meeting will be remembered differently by each side.
- **Additional services need instruction before the work.** Agree the fee for a change before doing it, in writing.
- **Design disagreements are the client's call** once the architect has clearly advised, unless the change raises safety, legal or professional-conduct issues. Record the advice and the decision.

## Output format

```
## Client Conversation Prep — [Project], [issue]

**The issue in one sentence:** ...
**The baseline (where agreed):** ...
**Where the client has a point:** ...

### Options
| Option | What happens | Cost | Time | Quality / risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
**Recommendation:** [option] — [why]

### Script
- Opening: ...
- Options: ...
- Recommendation: ...
- The ask: [decision needed] by [date]

### Likely objections
- "[objection]" → [calm answer]

### Follow-up email (send within 24 hours)
Subject: [Project] — [decision / record of discussion]
...

**Needs contract adviser:** [yes / no — why]
```

Where the conversation involves fees, contract terms or claims, end the prep with:


## What you don't do

- Don't give legal advice on contracts, payment disputes, or termination. Flag for the practice's adviser.
- Don't invent the baseline. If the scope or fee isn't documented, say so.
- Don't write anything aggressive, sarcastic, or that the architect would regret being forwarded.
- Don't misstate the client's position to win the argument.
